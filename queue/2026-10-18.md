# Literature Scout — 2026-10-18

Two candidates this week. Both are genuinely new data types relative to everything logged in
`INDEX.md`/`REJECTED.md` so far, and both come with real, stated reservations — neither is a
confident "this will work," and both need the project's permutation-test gauntlet before anything
else.

## Candidate 1: Foreign-ownership-limit ("FII headroom") scarcity premium

**Description:** A cross-sectional signal built from how much room is left for foreign portfolio
investors (FPIs) to buy a given Indian stock before hitting its regulatory Foreign Ownership
Limit (FOL — the sectoral FPI cap, since April 2020 set equal to the sector's FDI limit unless a
company has approved a lower cap). The idea: as foreign headroom shrinks toward zero, the
*effective float* available to a large and growing pool of foreign demand shrinks with it,
creating price pressure/segmentation independent of fundamentals — i.e., stocks running low on
foreign room should show elevated forward returns (scarcity-driven demand chasing a shrinking
tradeable float) until the cap binds, and potentially unusual dynamics (volatility, basis effects)
once it does.

**Source citations:**
- Regulatory mechanics: [NSDL Foreign Investment (FI) Limit Monitoring](https://issuer.nsdl.com/filimitmonitoring.html); [NSE India — Foreign investment limits](https://www.nseindia.com/static/market-data/foreign-investment-limit); FTSE Russell's own definition of "foreign headroom" = (FOL − foreign holdings)/FOL ([LSEG policy note](https://www.lseg.com/content/dam/ftse-russell/en_us/documents/policy-documents/foreign-ownership-restrictions-and-minimum-foreign-headroom-requirement.pdf)).
- Direct India anecdote: HDFC Bank's ADR traded at a >20% premium to its domestic shares once the
  bank's FII limit was fully exhausted, narrowing to ~11% after the limit was later raised
  ([Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/article/markets/foreign-portfolio-investor-legroom-gains-for-kotak-mahindra-bank-123012301357_1.html); [QuotedData, 2017](https://quoteddata.com/2017/12/jpmorgan-indian-loses-out-as-hdfc-foreign-premium-shrinks/)).
- Supporting academic framework: the closed-end country-fund segmentation literature (Bonser-Neal
  et al. 1990, *J. Finance*; summarized via [this review](https://fsrtest.galib.uga.edu/fsr/article/view/3663)) shows that binding foreign-ownership
  restrictions push country-fund prices away from NAV because markets become partially segmented —
  the same structural mechanism, one level removed (a fund wrapper vs. a single stock's own cap).
- A 2010 NIPFP working paper linked an India ADR-premium index to next-day net FPI purchase flows
  and found it compresses when the domestic Nifty rallies ([NIPFP WP 2010/71](https://www.nipfp.org.in/media/documents/wp_2010_71.pdf)) — again, evidence about
  the *ADR-vs-domestic basis*, not a domestic-only return-predictability test.

**Mechanism:** This is a limits-to-arbitrage / market-segmentation story, not a behavioral one: a
regulatory cap creates two distinct demand pools (foreign vs. domestic) competing for a float that
shrinks mechanically as the cap is approached, so price can diverge from fundamental value purely
from a supply/demand imbalance — directly analogous to the inelastic-markets price-pressure
mechanism already used for this project's 2026-10-04 index-reconstitution-flow candidate, except
triggered continuously by ownership headroom rather than a discrete index event.

**Evidence-strength assessment — honestly weak on the exact claim needed.** What's well
documented is the *ADR-domestic premium* (a basis/arbitrage phenomenon, and one this project
can't trade anyway since it's a long-only NSE domestic-equity book, not an ADR-arbitrage desk) and
the *closed-end-fund* analogy (a different instrument, foreign market). I could **not** find a
single academic study that tests "domestic NSE stock's forward return as a function of its own
foreign-headroom level/trend" as a cross-sectional factor — i.e., the exact claim this candidate
needs has not been tested by anyone, as far as I could find. This is explicitly a "plausible
mechanism, real anecdotal confirmation, zero direct academic backtest" situation — worth flagging
precisely because it's untested, not because it's proven.

**Why it might not transfer / confounds to watch:** Foreign ownership is currently near multi-year
lows in aggregate (FPI ownership in NSE-listed firms fell to 15.8% as of March 2026 per recent
reporting), so a binding-headroom event may now be a rare, concentrated-in-a-few-names phenomenon
(financials, insurance, a handful of consumer names) rather than a broad cross-sectional factor —
possible small-sample problem. There's also an obvious confound risk the project should
pre-register against: stocks that are *popular enough with foreign investors to be near their cap*
are very likely also large, liquid, well-covered blue chips — i.e., headroom-scarcity could simply
be relabeled large-cap/quality exposure (the mirror image of the small-cap confound that killed
the profitability factor), so a size-and-liquidity control is essential from the first test, not
an afterthought.

**Data needed / obtainability:** NSDL's FI Limit Monitoring page and NSE's static foreign-limits
page appear to show only the *current* snapshot per company, not a bulk historical point-in-time
series. A real backtest needs headroom-as-of-each-date history going back years. This has **not**
been checked the way the promoter-pledge data-availability check was done — treat sourcing this as
unverified and worth a dedicated data-availability check (comparable effort to the Trendlyne
pledge-data check in `data-sources/`) before committing to building a test.

## Candidate 2: Credit-rating-change momentum (downgrade/upgrade drift, not rating *level*)

**Description:** The Dichev & Piotroski (2001, *Journal of Finance*) finding that stocks
experience persistent abnormal returns for roughly a year *following* a bond-rating change — not
the announcement-day reaction, which is already priced in quickly, but a slow post-event drift,
with downgrades underperforming upgrades. This is a **different construct** from the
"credit-risk/rating anomaly" already logged as investigated-not-proposed on 2026-08-23 (that one
was a cross-sectional sort on the static *level* of credit risk/rating, which looked like the same
small-cap/illiquidity proxy that killed profitability). This candidate instead keys off the
*event* of a rating change and the drift that follows it — closer in spirit to PEAD, but for
credit-rating news instead of earnings news.

**Source citations:**
- Founding study (US): Dichev, I. & Piotroski, J. (2001), "The Long-Run Stock Returns Following
  Bond Ratings Changes," *Journal of Finance* ([copy](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/292280817_The_Long-Run_Stock_Returns_Following_Bond_Ratings_Changes)).
- EM replication, same sign: a Brazil study (Benjamin 2008, cited via secondary sources) and a
  later Brazilian paper covering 161 Moody's/S&P rating changes through 2018 using a
  Dichev-Piotroski-style methodology, both finding significant negative long-run abnormal returns
  after downgrades; a Russia study also reports substantial negative abnormal returns after
  downgrades.
- Confound/critical literature: Avramov, Chordia, Jostova & Philipov's "Anomalies and Financial
  Distress" line of work ([UNC copy](https://jhfinance.web.unc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/12369/2016/02/Jostova_anomalies.pdf); [Haas/Berkeley copy](https://haas.berkeley.edu/wp-content/uploads/bus.pdf)) finds that momentum-style and
  credit-risk-style strategies derive most of their profit from **shorting** high-credit-risk firms
  with deteriorating credit — i.e., the same distressed/illiquid subpopulation drives both the
  "level" anomaly and plausibly the "change" anomaly — and a related 2022 *Review of Finance*
  extension to corporate bonds reinforces that the anomaly survives but is entangled with
  limits-to-arbitrage and institutional-trading frictions in exactly the distressed names that are
  hardest to trade cleanly.
- India context found: Kaur & Dey (2024), "Price and Volume Effects Around Credit Rating Change
  Announcements: Evidence from India" ([SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/09721509241299952)) — but this is a short-window
  *announcement* event study (price/volume around the event), not a long-horizon post-event drift
  test, so it does not actually answer the question this candidate needs answered for India.

**Mechanism:** Underreaction to credit-relevant news — plausible given (a) India's retail- and
momentum-heavy market structure, and (b) a structural short-sale constraint that's even tighter
than the US's (only ~180-200 F&O-eligible NSE names can be shorted at all, already noted in this
project's 2026-09-13 entry on mutual-fund ownership breadth), which by the standard
differences-of-opinion logic should make downgrade-underreaction *harder* to arbitrage away in
India than in the US, if it transfers at all.

**Evidence-strength assessment:** Genuinely multi-market (US, Brazil, Russia, all same sign) — one
of the stronger replication records surfaced by this scout so far. But it comes with the project's
own house red flag already attached: the distress-anomaly literature's own authors show this
family of effects concentrates in the same small/illiquid/high-distress names each time, which is
structurally the same confound that collapsed the profitability factor once controlled for size
and beta. I'd rate this "real effect, high confound risk" rather than "real effect, clean" — it
should not be tested without a mandatory size/beta/liquidity control built in from the start, the
same discipline that caught the profitability-factor confound.

**Why it might not transfer / what's missing for India specifically:** No long-horizon India study
found — only the short-window Kaur & Dey event study, which is a different question. India's
corporate-bond-rating ecosystem (CRISIL, ICRA, India Ratings, CARE) rates far more issuers than
have liquid listed equity, so there may be a smaller usable sample of rating-change events mapped
to liquid NSE names than the Brazil/Russia studies had.

**Data needed / obtainability:** Point-in-time company-level rating-change history (agency, date,
old rating, new rating) for NSE-listed issuers. Rating agencies publish individual press releases
and aggregate "default and transition study" PDFs (CRISIL publishes one annually), but I found no
evidence of a bulk, machine-readable, point-in-time rating-action feed comparable to what this
project's XBRL collector does for fundamentals — this would likely be a scrape-and-structure
project of its own, comparable in effort to the promoter-pledge or e-way-bill data-building
exercises already flagged in prior runs, not a quick add.

## Investigated, not proposed this week

**Analyst coverage initiation/termination ("neglected stock" via coverage, not news-count).**
Distinct literature (Demiroglu & Ryngaert 2010; Scherbina-adjacent work on dropped coverage) from
the 2026-08-30 media-attention candidate, but on inspection it's the same underlying
limited-attention mechanism just operationalized through sell-side analyst coverage instead of
news-count — not independent evidence. The clean abnormal return in the literature (~4.9% in
Demiroglu & Ryngaert) sits mostly at the *initiation announcement itself*, which is an event
reaction, not a standing cross-sectional factor — the same "decays to near-zero once you need it to
survive as an ongoing factor rather than a one-time pop" problem already flagged for PEAD. No
India-specific study found. Not pursued further.
