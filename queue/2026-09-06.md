# Literature Scout — 2026-09-06

Two candidates this week, both distinct data types from everything currently open or
rejected in `INDEX.md` / `REJECTED.md`. Both are ownership/structural or labor-market
data, not price/technical, not profitability, not PEAD, and not any of the flow/insider/
attention/pledge/lock-up ideas already on the queue.

---

## Candidate 1: Cross-holding / "HoldCo discount-to-NAV" mean reversion

**Description.** Buy (or overweight) listed Indian holding companies whose market
capitalization trades at the widest discount to the sum-of-the-parts (SOTP) market value
of their listed subsidiary stakes, on the thesis that unusually wide discounts partially
mean-revert. This is a *relative, asset-based* value construct — NAV of holdings vs. the
holding vehicle's own market cap — not an earnings- or book-value-based value signal like
the E/P candidate already on the internal queue.

**Sources.**
- Academic backbone (analogous instrument, not India-specific): the closed-end fund (CEF)
  discount-to-NAV literature is one of the most replicated anomalies in finance — e.g.
  Pontiff (1995, *J. Finance*), and more recent work such as Niklas Kohl, ["Closed-End Fund
  Abnormal Returns and Discount Mean-Reversion"](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2294410)
  (SSRN) and the review at
  ["Evidence on the Mean-Reverting Tendencies of Closed-End Fund Discounts"](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/finrev/v38y2003i2p273-291.html)
  (*Financial Review*, 2003). This literature documents that CEF discounts are large,
  persistent, mean-reverting, and that strategies buying wide-discount funds and shorting
  or avoiding narrow-discount/premium funds earn returns in excess of standard risk
  factors, with mean reversion driven by both price and NAV moves.
- India-specific descriptive evidence (practitioner/valuation research, not peer-reviewed
  academic finance): an HDFC Securities sell-side note on
  ["Holdcos for portfolio diversification"](https://www.hdfcsec.com/hsl.docs/Holdcos%20for%20portfolio%20diversification%20-%20HSIE-202112021525595803421.pdf),
  an independent valuation firm's ["Study on Holdco Discount 2024"](https://incwert.com/holdco-discount-2024/)
  reporting a median ~68% discount to NAV across major listed Indian holding entities in
  FY24, and multiple financial-media pieces (e.g. Business Standard's
  ["18 top holdco shares gain 40% so far this year, twice that of Sensex"](https://www.business-standard.com/article/companies/18-top-holdco-shares-gain-40-so-far-this-year-twice-that-of-sensex-121120500633_1.html))
  documenting episodes where discount narrowing drove outsized returns, and a specific
  case study (Bajaj Holdings & Investment Ltd, whose discount reportedly narrowed from
  ~60% to ~49% over FY20–FY24 as its main holding, Bajaj Finance, re-rated).

**Mechanism.** Two candidate explanations, both plausible and not mutually exclusive: (1)
a structural/frictional discount — double taxation of dividends flowing up through the
holding layer, holding-company overhead, illiquidity of the holdco's own stock relative to
its underlying holdings, and index exclusion (many holdcos sit outside major indices,
reducing passive/institutional demand) — that is fairly stable but occasionally
overshoots and then reverts; and (2) an agency/complexity discount reflecting investor
skepticism that promoter families holding these vehicles will ever actually
monetize/unlock the value (dividend the underlying stakes out or restructure), which
narrows episodically around specific corporate actions (demergers, buybacks, block
divestment of subsidiary stakes, SEBI's 2024 special call-auction framework for thinly
traded/wide-discount securities). This is economically distinct from the E/P or
profitability factors: it isn't about earnings quality or valuation multiples on the
firm's own income statement, it's a structural mispricing of a wrapper around
already-priced assets.

**Evidence-strength assessment.** Mixed, and I want to be explicit about the mixing:
the *mechanism and mean-reversion property* have strong, decades-deep, multi-market
academic replication — but that replication is in the closed-end fund literature, not in
operating holding companies. I could not find a peer-reviewed academic paper that runs a
cross-sectional backtest of "buy the widest-discount Indian holdcos" and reports a
return/Sharpe number; what exists on the India side is sell-side/valuation-practitioner
description of the phenomenon (real, large, and persistent — 60–80% discounts are
reported repeatedly across independent sources) plus anecdotal case studies of discount
narrowing coinciding with strong stock performance, which is suggestive but not a
controlled test (discount narrowing and strong subsidiary performance are close to the
same event, so these anecdotes don't cleanly separate "the discount reverted" from
"the subsidiary re-rated and the holdco went along for the ride at a lagged beta"). Also
worth flagging up front: the tradable universe of genuine India holdcos with liquid,
mostly-listed subsidiary stakes (Bajaj Holdings, Tata Investment Corp, Bombay Burmah,
Kalyani Investment, a handful of others) is small — plausibly 20–40 names — which is a
breadth problem for a family-wise permutation test, similar to the F&O-universe cap
already flagged for the options-skew candidate on the open queue.

**Why it might/might not transfer to India.** It doesn't need to "transfer" in the sense
of a foreign result being ported — the phenomenon is already directly observed in India,
arguably more strongly than in developed markets (discounts of 60-80% vs. a more typical
~20% for foreign holdcos, per the sources above), plausibly because promoter-family
control and weak minority-shareholder monetization pressure are more entrenched here than
in markets with more shareholder activism. The open question is not "does the discount
exist" (it clearly does) but "is the discount's *width* cross-sectionally predictive of
*subsequent relative returns*, net of the subsidiaries' own factor exposures" — that is
the part that would need to be built and tested from scratch, since no one seems to have
published it.

**Data needed to test on India-listed equities.** For each candidate holdco: (a) its
disclosed shareholding in each subsidiary/associate (already-collected Reg. 31
shareholding-pattern data used for the promoter-pledge candidate would give this for
listed subsidiaries), (b) daily market cap of each listed subsidiary/associate to compute
SOTP NAV, (c) the holdco's own daily market cap, (d) some treatment for any unlisted or
minority-stake assets (net cash, real estate, unlisted investments) that will need a
book-value or otherwise conservative fallback since they have no market price. This is
mechanically simple (market-cap arithmetic, not fundamentals modeling) but the universe
is small and each name needs manual verification of its holding structure — this is a
bespoke, hand-curated dataset of ~20-40 names rather than something that scales the way
the point-in-time fundamentals pipeline does.

NOT YET TESTED — requires the project's permutation-test gauntlet before any deployment
consideration.

## Candidate 2: Labor hiring / employee-growth as an investment-based return predictor

**Description.** Firms with high recent hiring/labor-expansion rates earn systematically
*lower* subsequent returns than firms with low or negative hiring rates, controlling for
other characteristics — the reverse sign of what a naive "growth is good" intuition would
suggest. This would be a new data type for the project (labor/employee-cost data from
company filings), distinct from profitability, technicals, and everything currently on
the queue.

**Source.** Frederico Belo, Xiaoji Lin, Santiago Bazdresch,
["Labor Hiring, Investment, and Stock Return Predictability in the Cross Section,"](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1267462)
*Journal of Political Economy*, 2014, Vol. 122, No. 1, pp. 129–177 (top-tier journal). As
reported in the paper and its summaries: in the cross-section of US firms, a 10
percentage-point increase in a firm's hiring rate is associated with roughly a 1.5
percentage-point *decrease* in its subsequent annual risk premium; the effect is reported
to survive controls for other known return predictors, and is described as significantly
steeper in industries that rely more on high-skill labor.

**Mechanism.** This is presented as a *risk-based*, not behavioral, explanation: hiring
and firing have real adjustment costs (search, training, severance, disruption), so firms
that have recently expanded headcount have taken on more of this "labor adjustment cost"
exposure. In the model, a positive shock that lowers adjustment costs economy-wide
benefits high-hiring firms disproportionately, so their equity acts as a hedge against
that risk and consequently earns a lower risk premium in equilibrium (the same logic
underlying the broader investment/asset-growth literature: expanding firms are lower-risk,
lower-expected-return, all else equal). This is a genuine economic mechanism, not a
pattern with no rationale, but it is worth being explicit that it sits in the same family
as the well-known investment/asset-growth factor (CMA in Fama-French 5-factor, the q-factor
model) — it is not a wholly independent idea, it's a labor-specific refinement of a
broader "investment predicts returns negatively" result.

**Evidence-strength assessment.** Strong on the "is this a real, replicated finding"
axis for the US: top journal, well-cited, and the broader investment/asset-growth
literature it belongs to has been replicated across many markets. Weak on the
"does it hold and does it transfer to India" axis: I found no India-specific or
broader-emerging-market replication of the labor-hiring result specifically (as opposed
to the more general investment/asset-growth factor, which I also did not find a citable
India-specific test of, but is a different candidate from this one). I want to flag
explicitly the confound risk this project has already learned to check for: this
project's profitability postmortem found that a seemingly real factor collapsed into a
small-cap/high-beta risk proxy once properly controlled. Hiring rate is very plausibly
correlated with firm size and growth-stage (small/young firms hire faster in percentage
terms), so an India test of this would need the same size/beta controls applied to
profitability, not just a naive univariate sort, before trusting any result.

**Why it might/might not transfer to India.** No strong prior either way. Arguments for
transfer: India has a young, still-formalizing corporate labor market with plausibly
*higher* average adjustment costs (skilled-labor scarcity in specific sectors, stricter
labor law frictions around retrenchment than the US) which could make the effect
stronger, not weaker, here. Arguments against: the effect in the source paper is
described as strongest in high-skill-labor industries in a US context (tech, professional
services); India's listed-company sectoral mix is different (financials, energy,
materials, IT-services being a partial exception), and the size/beta confound above is a
real risk given this project's own prior finding.

**Data needed to test on India-listed equities.** This needs a genuine caveat on data
availability, not just "yes, obtainable." The paper's ideal measure is *number of
employees* year-over-year. Actual employee headcount is not a standard line item in
Indian XBRL financial statements (income-statement XBRL, which this project already
collects from ~2018, carries "Employee benefits expense" as a P&L line, not headcount).
Real headcount disclosure in India has historically been inconsistent — some companies
report it in the Directors' Report or Annual Report text, and more recently the mandatory
Business Responsibility and Sustainability Report (BRSR, required for the top 1,000
listed companies by market cap only from around FY2022–23 onward) includes workforce
metrics. So there are two realistic options, each imperfect: (a) use employee benefits
expense growth as a proxy for hiring, which is readily available from the existing XBRL
pipeline back to ~2018 but conflates headcount growth with wage inflation and
skill-mix changes, or (b) use BRSR headcount data for a true hiring-rate measure, which is
more faithful to the original paper but only available for a subset of large-cap names
and only from FY22-23 onward, giving a much shorter and narrower sample than the
project's other candidates. Either path is buildable, but this is not a "point-and-run"
signal against the existing XBRL collector the way profit trend or F-Score are.

NOT YET TESTED — requires the project's permutation-test gauntlet before any deployment
consideration.

## Investigated but not proposed this week

**Securities lending / short interest (NSE SLB scheme) as a negative return predictor.**
This is a genuinely new data type and has strong international academic support (short
interest negatively predicts returns — see Asquith, Pathak & Ritter, NBER w10434, and the
broader international-evidence literature, e.g. Oxford's *Review of Asset Pricing
Studies* 13(4), 691, 2024). Not proposing because feasibility looks weak specifically for
India: multiple sources describe NSE's Securities Lending and Borrowing (SLB) mechanism as
thin/inactive relative to developed markets (one paper we found attributes part of the
success of India's single-stock-futures market precisely to "the absence of an efficient
or active stock-lending mechanism" in Indian equities) — if the underlying lending market
itself is illiquid and narrow, any short-interest series built from it is unlikely to have
the breadth or reliability needed for a systematic cross-sectional signal. Flagging so a
future run doesn't re-open this without first confirming SLB volume/coverage has
materially improved.

**Related-party-transaction (RPT) volume / "tunneling" as a direct return-predicting
factor.** The economic case for RPTs as an India-specific expropriation channel is
genuinely well established — Bertrand, Mehta & Mullainathan,
["Ferreting Out Tunneling: An Application to Indian Business Groups,"](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/qjecon/v117y2002i1p121-148..html)
*Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 2002, is a landmark, highly-cited paper using Indian
business-group data specifically. However, on close reading this paper (and the broader
RPT literature we could find, e.g. surveys via ResearchGate/ScienceDirect on RPTs and firm
performance) documents that tunneling *happens* and *depresses measured firm
performance/value* — it does not document that RPT intensity or the tunneling
methodology's output cross-sectionally *predicts future stock returns* in a way a
systematic strategy could trade. That is a meaningfully different (and harder) claim, and
we could not find a study making it. Not proposing as a standalone return factor this
week; distinct from — and a plausible complement to, if it were built — the promoter-
pledge and promoter/insider-transaction candidates already open on the queue, since
mandatory RPT disclosure (Companies Act 2013 / SEBI LODR) is a different data source than
either of those.

**Corporate governance quality scores (e.g. IiAS Governance Scorecard) as a return
factor.** Rejected quickly: IiAS's own published materials state directly that a high
governance score "is not an indicator of current or future financial performance, or
stock price performance." Broader international evidence (an MSCI emerging-markets
governance study) found that higher governance-pillar scores were associated with
outperformance in developed markets but explicitly *not* in emerging markets. Given both
the source's own disclaimer and the EM-specific null finding, this looks like the same
kind of result as the BRSR/ESG null already recorded in REJECTED-adjacent notes — not
proposing.

**Patent-based "innovative efficiency" (patents granted per R&D dollar).** Hirshleifer,
Hsu & Li, *Journal of Financial Economics* 107(3), 632–654, 2013, is a strong, well-cited
US result with a plausible limited-attention/mispricing mechanism. Not proposing for
India this week for the same reason the GST e-way-bill idea was set aside previously: no
India-specific replication exists, and building the underlying dataset (matching listed
companies to patent filings and constructing an R&D-capital-adjusted efficiency measure)
would be a from-scratch data-engineering effort with real coverage-bias risk, since
domestic Indian patent filing is heavily concentrated in a handful of sectors
(pharma, IT, auto components) rather than broad-based across the listed universe.
