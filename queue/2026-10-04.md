# Literature Scout — 2026-10-04

Two candidates this week, both genuinely new data types relative to the 8 prior runs logged in
`INDEX.md`/`REJECTED.md` (which between them already span options-implied signals, promoter/insider
transactions and pledging, FII/DII and mutual-fund ownership and flows, analyst estimates and
dispersion, attention/media, customer-supplier networks, filing-timeliness, HoldCo discounts, labor

## Candidate 1: Asset growth / investment anomaly (low-investment firms outperform)

**Description:** A cross-sectional characteristic sorting firms by year-over-year total-asset growth
(or capex intensity). Low-asset-growth firms have historically outperformed high-asset-growth firms.
This is an *investment*-based construct, distinct from the *profitability*-based factors (gross
profitability, ROE, ROA, margins) this project already tested and rejected — profitability asks "how
much does the firm earn," this asks "how much is the firm plowing back into growing itself."

**Sources:**
- Cooper, Gulen & Schill, "Asset Growth and the Cross-Section of Stock Returns," *Journal of Finance*
  63(4), 2008, pp. 1609-1651. https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2008.01370.x
  (Note: an earlier-stage version of this brief mis-cited this paper's authorship; corrected here.)
- Titman, Wei & Xie, "Capital Investments and Stock Returns," *Journal of Financial and Quantitative
  Analysis* 39(4), 2004, pp. 677-700.
  https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=4158&context=lkcsb_research ,
  https://www.nber.org/papers/w9951
- Watanabe, Xu, Yao & Yu, "The Asset Growth Effect: Insights from International Equity Markets,"
  *Journal of Financial Economics* 108(2), 2013, pp. 529-563.
  https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2222295 (full text blocked by this session's
  egress proxy; summary below relies on search-result abstract, not a verified full read — flagging
  this explicitly, including that I could not independently confirm whether India specifically was
  one of the 40 countries in their panel)
- Fama & French, "Dissecting Anomalies," *Journal of Finance* 63(4), 2008, pp. 1653-1678.
  https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2008.01371.x
- India-specific data point: Agarwalla, Jacob & Varma, "Four Factors Model in Indian Equities Market"
  (IIM Ahmedabad working paper; NSE data, Oct 1993-Dec 2013).
  https://faculty.iima.ac.in/iffm/Indian-Fama-French-Momentum/four-factors-India-90s-onwards-IIM-WP-Version.pdf

**Mechanism (source claims):** Two competing explanations in the literature, and they are not a minor
footnote — they disagree about what kind of effect this even is. Cooper, Gulen & Schill, and Titman,
Wei & Xie, argue it's **behavioral/agency-based mispricing**: investors over-extrapolate recent growth
rates, and/or managers with more investment discretion (cash-rich, low-leverage, weak governance)
overinvest in low-return projects ("empire-building"), which the market underreacts to. Watanabe, Xu,
Yao & Yu push back with an **optimal-investment (q-theory), risk-based** reading: they find the
negative asset-growth/return relation is *stronger*, not weaker, in more developed and efficiently
priced markets — the opposite of what a limits-to-arbitrage/mispricing story would predict — and read
that as evidence the effect reflects firms rationally investing more when their cost of capital/
discount rate is lower, not mispricing. I am not resolving that dispute; it means this candidate
doesn't come with a single settled "why," which is itself worth weighing against a factor with a
single clean rationale.

**My assessment of evidence strength:** More than a single-study curiosity — Watanabe et al. report
the relation holding across 40 countries, a real multi-market replication bar few prior candidates in
this queue have cleared. But two things materially weaken it for this project's purposes:
1. **The same confound shape that killed profitability.** Fama & French (2008) directly report the
   asset-growth effect is concentrated in microcap and small-stock groups and essentially
   insignificant for big stocks. That is structurally the identical pattern this project found when
   profitability coefficients collapsed under size/beta controls (|t|<2) while size alone stayed
   significant. This isn't a confound I'm speculating about — it's stated directly in the anomaly's
   own foundational literature. Any India test of this needs size-bucketed sorts and an explicit
   beta control from the first pass, not as a follow-up robustness check.
2. **Weak, marginal India evidence so far.** The closest existing India data point is not a
   dedicated asset-growth study but the CMA (investment) factor inside a standard Fama-French
   five-factor replication for NSE: Agarwalla, Jacob & Varma report a CMA premium of roughly
   0.22%/month with t≈1.99 — borderline significant and smaller than the size and profitability
   factors in the same sample. That is a thin starting point, not a confirmed India anomaly.

**Why it might or might not transfer to India:** The mechanism dispute matters here specifically
because this project's own profitability postmortem landed on the "disguised small-cap/beta premium"
side of an analogous question. If the behavioral/agency story is right, the effect should be strongest
exactly where it would be hardest to distinguish from the already-rejected size/beta confound
(illiquid, weakly governed small caps — a very large share of the NSE universe). If the
optimal-investment story is right, the effect should hold or strengthen in large, liquid names — which
would make it a genuinely distinct, confound-resistant signal. The India CMA evidence currently
available doesn't settle which world we're in.

**Data needed for an India test, and a real blocker:** Needs year-over-year (ideally point-in-time)
total-asset figures from two or more consecutive annual balance sheets per firm, plus the capex/PP&E
series for the Titman-Wei-Xie variant. This project's XBRL collector only has balance-sheet history
from roughly 2023 onward — that gives at most 1-2 years of asset-growth observations today, far short
of what the founding studies use (20-35 years, Fama-MacBeth cross-sections). This candidate is
**currently data-blocked**, not just untested — it cannot get a defensible backtest until the
balance-sheet history either accumulates further or is backfilled from pre-2023 annual reports. It
should sit on the queue as "pending data extension," re-tested with size/beta controls built in from
the start, rather than treated as immediately actionable.

NOT YET TESTED — requires the project's permutation-test gauntlet before any deployment consideration.

## Candidate 2: Index reconstitution flows — narrowly, deletion rebound and pre-announcement candidate screening (NOT the classic announcement-day effect)

**Description:** When NSE adds or removes a stock from a widely tracked index (Nifty 50, Nifty Next
50, sectoral indices), passive funds are mechanically forced to buy or sell, creating demand-shock
price pressure. This is a new data type for this project (index-membership/passive-flow events,
distinct from everything else investigated so far), but the honest framing matters: the obvious,
well-known version of this signal — trading the announcement-day or effective-date price move itself —
looks dead on the evidence, both globally and in India. What's left, and the only part of this I'm
actually proposing, is two narrower variants: (a) buying names shortly after their forced-sale
deletion overshoot, once the mechanical selling pressure fades, and (b) screening Nifty Next 50 names
that are probable *upcoming* promotion candidates by NSE's own published methodology (market cap/
turnover rank), ahead of the official announcement.

**Sources:**
- Chen, Noronha & Singal, "The Price Response to S&P 500 Index Additions and Deletions: Evidence of
  Asymmetry and a New Explanation," *Journal of Finance* 59(4), 2004, pp. 1901-1930.
- Greenwood & Sammon, "The Disappearing Index Effect," NBER Working Paper 30748, 2022 (published
  *Journal of Finance*, April 2025). https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w30748/w30748.pdf
- Marisetty, "Market Reaction to Index Reconstitutions: Evidence from NSE NIFTY Additions and
  Deletions," SSRN working paper, Oct 2025. https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5642110
- "Impact on Stock Price by the Inclusion to and Exclusion from CNX Nifty Index."
  https://www.researchgate.net/publication/254093342
- NSE index methodology and reconstitution press releases (data-source reference, not a finding):
  https://nsearchives.nseindia.com/content/indices/Method_NIFTY_Equity_Indices.pdf ,
  niftyindices.com/Press_Release

**Mechanism (source claims):** Pure mechanical demand-shock, not information or behavioral — index
funds and ETFs must hold index constituents in proportion to their index weight, so an addition forces
buying and a deletion forces selling, independent of any news about firm fundamentals. Chen, Noronha &
Singal's documented asymmetry (additions leave a more permanent price bump; deletions are followed by
partial reversal) is their own proposed explanation for why the two sides of the same mechanical flow
don't behave symmetrically.

**My assessment of evidence strength, and why the headline version doesn't make the cut:** The
announcement/effective-date price-pop itself has been shown, in both the US (Greenwood & Sammon 2025:
S&P 500 inclusion effect fell from +7.6% in the 1990s to +0.8% in 2010-2020) and in India (Marisetty
2025, using 2010-2024 NSE data, finds the effect weakening across sub-periods, consistent with the US
decay pattern; an older CNX Nifty study concludes outright that gains "fail to sustain" and that it is
"impossible to earn excess returns"), to be heavily arbitraged away by index-arbitrage desks that
trade the announcement within minutes. Given NSE's own mandatory 4-week minimum notice before an
effective date — more lead time than the US's roughly one-week gap — I would be surprised if any
edge survives in the classic form for a strategy that isn't trading the announcement itself. I am
flagging that candidly rather than proposing the obvious version of this idea. The narrower variants
(deletion-rebound, pre-announcement candidate screening) have much thinner direct evidentiary support
— mostly the "partial recovery" language in the deletion studies above, plus NSE's own published,
deterministic selection methodology for (b) rather than a dedicated backtested paper — so this
candidate's evidence strength is genuinely weaker than Candidate 1's; I want that asymmetry visible
rather than presenting both as equally solid.

**Why it might or might not transfer to India, and a real power constraint:** The mechanism is at
least as strong in India as in the US — Nifty-tracking passive AUM has grown substantially and NSE's
own long notice period should, if anything, make pre-announcement candidate screening *more* viable
here than in markets with shorter notice windows (more time to position ahead of the forced flow).
Against that: there are only roughly 8-15 qualifying Nifty 50/Next 50 add/delete events per year,
which is a real statistical-power constraint for any backtest and means this would operate as a small
satellite/event-driven sleeve rather than a continuously re-ranked, broad cross-sectional factor like
this project's other candidates.

**Data needed for an India test:** NSE and BSE publish dated index-methodology documents and
reconstitution press releases/circulars with fixed review dates (semi-annual cutoffs late Jan/July,
effective dates end of March/September, plus occasional interim changes) — this is realistically
scrapeable and point-in-time clean, comparable in difficulty to corporate-action data already handled
elsewhere in this project, though it means archiving dated PDFs/circulars rather than pulling one clean
historical CSV.

NOT YET TESTED — requires the project's permutation-test gauntlet before any deployment consideration.

## Investigated, not proposed this week

- **Open-market share buyback announcements as a long-horizon drift signal.** The classic finding
  (Ikenberry, Lakonishok & Vermaelen 1995, US: ~12% average 4-year abnormal return after announcement,
  rising to ~45% in the "value" subsample) has a plausible undervaluation-signaling mechanism, but
  even in its home market the long-run drift has reportedly decayed to near zero for 2003-2012
  repurchases (Fu & Huang 2016) as the strategy became known. For India, I found no credible
  long-horizon, factor-controlled replication — only short-window announcement-CAR event studies with
  no size/value/momentum controls, which leaves the same kind of small-cap/distress confound this
  project's profitability postmortem flagged, entirely unaddressed. More decisively: SEBI phased out
  open-market buybacks via the stock-exchange route entirely as of April 2025 (a 2023 amendment glide
  path), pushing essentially all recent Indian buybacks to tender offers — a mechanically different
  instrument, not the same signaling/underreaction setup. SEBI is reportedly proposing to reinstate
  open-market buybacks from August 2026. Parked rather than proposed: the India mechanism this
  candidate depends on was not even operating for most of 2025-2026, so there isn't a sample to build a
  case on yet. Worth revisiting once (a) open-market buybacks resume and (b) enough post-resumption
  data accumulates for even a preliminary look.
- **Google Search Volume Index (SVI) / Google Trends retail attention.** Mechanistically distinct from
  the news-coverage-count attention candidate already sitting in this queue from the 2026-08-30 run
  (Da, Engelberg & Gao 2011 frame SVI as *retail* demand-driven order-flow pressure — Barber & Odean's
  attention-buying story — versus Fang & Peress's *institutional*-neglect/information-diffusion
  mechanism for news coverage), so not simple duplication. There is India-specific evidence: Aziz &
  Ansari (2021, *Vision*, 93 Indian stocks 2012-2017) find Google search predicts a 1-2 week excess
  return that reverses within the following week, explicitly invoking the same retail price-pressure
  mechanism. But that pattern is the problem: it is a short-horizon overreaction-then-reversal, not a
  persistent premium, which sits awkwardly in a long-only pipeline built around standing cross-sectional
  factors — it would need to be implemented as a fast-turnover fade/avoid overlay, not a standard entry
  signal, which is a different kind of project than what this scout's brief is sized for. On top of
  that, Google Trends has no official bulk historical API with guaranteed point-in-time snapshots and
  is documented to retroactively rescale historical values on re-query, with worse sampling noise for
  thinly-searched small/mid-cap tickers — a serious, independent data-integrity obstacle on top of the
  implementation mismatch. Not proposed this week; flagged as needing a from-scratch redesign (as a
  risk overlay, not a factor) before it would be worth a human's time.

Also noted in passing (not separately diligenced in depth this week): the Fu & Huang (2016) and
Greenwood & Sammon (2025) "effect decays once arbitraged" pattern that shows up in both candidates
above (buybacks, index effect) is worth keeping in mind as a general caution for future scout runs —
anomalies with a long US track record are not automatically still live, even before asking whether
they transfer to India at all.
