## Candidate 1: Composite/net share issuance anomaly (equity issuance predicts low future returns)

**Description:** Firms that issue net new equity (follow-on offerings, rights issues,
QIPs, preferential allotments, ESOP-driven dilution, convertible conversion) over a
trailing window subsequently underperform firms that are net equity *repurchasers* or
issue nothing. The standard construction (Daniel & Titman 2006; Pontiff & Woodgate
2008) is "composite issuance": the growth in shares outstanding not explained by stock
splits/bonus issues, split into a repurchase leg and an issuance leg.

**Source citations:**
- Sehgal, S. & Pandey, A. (2013), "An Empirical Investigation of the Relationship
  between Net Stock Issues and Returns in India," *India-specific, direct firm-level
  test*. (https://www.pollux-fid.de/r/cr-10.1177/0258042x13514888)
- Long, H., Chiah, M., Zaremba, A. & Umar, Z. (2023), "Composite equity issuance and
  the cross-section of country and industry returns," *Applied Economics* —
  68 markets, 1976–2022.
- Daniel, K. & Titman, S. (2006), "Market Reactions to Tangible and Intangible
  Information," *Journal of Finance* (US founding paper, composite issuance measure).
- Pontiff, J. & Woodgate, A. (2008), "Share Issuance and Cross-Sectional Returns,"
  *Journal of Finance* (US; I recall from training knowledge, not verified this run,
  that this paper's main contribution was showing the effect survives value-weighting
  and is not purely a microcap artifact — **flagging this as unverified**, see
  Evidence-strength below).

**Mechanism (one paragraph):** Two complementary explanations are offered in the
literature. (1) Market-timing/agency: managers have an informational advantage over
outside investors about their own firm's prospects and opportunistically issue equity
when shares are overvalued (and repurchase when undervalued), so issuance is a noisy
but real signal of insider-perceived overvaluation. (2) Investment-based/risk-based:
issuance finances investment, and investment-heavy firms subsequently have lower risk
and lower expected returns (the same family of argument as the asset-growth anomaly
already on this project's queue from 2026-10-04). Both mechanisms point the same
direction (issuers underperform), which is part of why the effect is considered robust,
but it also means it is not fully independent of the asset-growth story already
flagged.

**Evidence-strength assessment:**
- *My assessment, not a confirmed fact*: this is NOT single-study evidence — the
  country/industry-level version has very broad (68-market) multi-decade replication,
  and the firm-level version has a dedicated India test (Sehgal & Pandey) that finds
  the same sign. That combination is stronger than most candidates surfaced in prior
  runs.
- *Caveat I could not resolve this session*: the 68-market Long et al. (2023) result is
  at the **country/industry aggregate level**, not firm-level cross-sectional — it shows
  that countries/industries which issue heavily subsequently underperform other
  countries/industries, which is a different (related but not identical) claim from
  "stock-picking by issuance within India outperforms." The firm-level claim for India
  rests on the single Sehgal & Pandey (2013) paper, which I was only able to read via
  abstract/summary, not the full text — I could not verify effect size, sample period,
  or whether it controls for size/beta the way this project's own profitability
  postmortem would demand. I also could not verify my recollection that Pontiff &
  Woodgate (2008) shows the effect survives value-weighting; if that recollection is
  wrong, this candidate has the exact same small-cap/beta-proxy failure mode that killed
  the profitability factor, and that check should be the first thing done before any
  backtest.
- Plausible mechanism: yes (market timing is a well-accepted, non-mysterious
  behavioral/informational story, not a data-mined pattern with no rationale).
- Known-confound risk: **real and explicitly flagged** — overlaps partially with (a)
  the asset-growth/investment anomaly already queued (2026-10-04) on the investment
  side, and (b) the open-market-buyback candidate already investigated
  (2026-10-04, found decayed/banned-in-India) on the repurchase side. A full
  Daniel-Titman composite-issuance construction nets these two legs together, which is
  awkward for India specifically because open-market buybacks were banned outright from
  2023 until a reported Aug-2026 reinstatement — during that window the "composite"
  measure collapses to just the issuance leg, so the measure's meaning shifts
  discontinuously across the sample period. **If pursued, the simpler net-issuance
  (not full composite) construction used by Sehgal & Pandey is probably the more
  honest fit for India's market structure, at least until buybacks are active again.**

**Why it might/might not transfer to India:** For: SEOs, QIPs, rights issues and
preferential allotments are common and well-documented financing events in India, and
the single existing India study finds the same-sign relationship as the US/international
literature, so this isn't a case of "US-only, never checked elsewhere." Against: India's
primary-market structure differs from the US (QIPs and preferential allotments to
promoters/anchor investors are much more common than classic rights-to-all-shareholders
SEOs), so the US intuition about retail/market mispricing around a public offering may
not map cleanly onto a QIP placed with institutional anchors at a negotiated price — the
"market is surprised and systematically wrong" story is weaker when the buyer is a
sophisticated institution pricing the deal itself.

**Data needed to test on India:** Share-outstanding history per company (to compute net
issuance net of splits/bonuses — this is standard NSE/BSE corporate-action data, already
routinely collected by data vendors), plus a bonus/split-adjustment factor to avoid
false-positive "issuance" from stock splits. This is NOT blocked by the project's
current XBRL collector's 2018-income-statement/2023-balance-sheet limitation, since
shares outstanding and corporate actions are market-structure data, not accounting
data — this is a genuine advantage over the asset-growth candidate, which is explicitly
stuck waiting on balance-sheet history.

**NOT YET TESTED — requires the project's permutation-test gauntlet before any
deployment consideration.**

## Candidate 2: Beneish M-score (earnings-manipulation-risk screen) as a return predictor

**Description:** The Beneish M-score is an 8-ratio composite (days-sales-in-receivables
index, gross-margin index, asset-quality index, sales-growth index, depreciation index,
SG&A index, leverage index, total-accruals-to-total-assets) originally built to flag
firms with a high probability of having manipulated earnings. The candidate here is not
fraud detection itself but the downstream claim that firms flagged as likely manipulators
(M-score above the -1.78 threshold) subsequently earn lower returns, i.e. the market
under-reacts to manipulation risk and the market corrects (or the manipulation itself
unwinds) over the following year.

**Source citations:**
- Jain, G. & Bose, A. (2023), "Are Returns Conditional Upon the Beneish M-score?",
  *Academy of Marketing Studies Journal*, Vol. 27(3) — NSE 500, 10 years of data.
  (https://www.abacademies.org/articles/are-returns-conditional-upon-the-beneish-m-score-15680.html)
- Beneish, M. D. (1999), "The Detection of Earnings Manipulation," *Financial Analysts
  Journal* — original US construction and threshold.
- GMT Research, practitioner note on applying Beneish M-score to Asian/Indian
  accounting disclosure conventions (COGS/SG&A split issue — see caveat below).
  (https://www.gmtresearch.com/?p=5056)

**Mechanism (one paragraph):** The story is behavioral/informational, not a risk
premium: management has private information about the true quality of reported
earnings; a high M-score is a public, mechanically-computable proxy for that hidden
quality problem; if the market doesn't fully price this proxy on publication of
financial statements, flagged firms should underperform as the manipulation is detected,
reverses, or the firm's true (weaker) economics reassert themselves. This is a distinct
mechanism from the profitability-factor story (level of profitability as a quality/risk
signal) — it's about the *reliability* of reported numbers, not their level.

**Evidence-strength assessment — this one is weak, and I want to say so plainly rather
than pass it through:**
- Single India study (Jain & Bose 2023). I could not confirm from the abstract/summary
  whether the reported "lower returns" are raw returns, size-adjusted, or
  risk-adjusted, nor the actual spread magnitude — the search only surfaced that the
  effect exists and "varies from year to year" by the authors' own admission. That
  "varies from one year to another" line is itself a flag: it suggests the result may
  not be stable, which is exactly the kind of thing this project's own permutation-test
  gauntlet exists to catch.
- A second India paper (Chopra & Miglani 2018, IIM Calcutta) computes abnormal returns
  for M-score-flagged vs unflagged portfolios but I could not determine the direction or
  significance of its result from available summaries — genuinely unresolved, not
  glossed over.
- Construction risk specific to India: GMT Research's practitioner note states the
  Beneish ratios (which depend on a clean COGS/SG&A split) often can't be computed
  cleanly for Asian issuers, including Indian ones, that don't disclose that split the
  way US GAAP filers do — this is a real data-quality risk, not a hypothetical one,
  and would need to be checked against what the project's own XBRL collector actually
  captures before this is testable at all.
- Overlap with a known rejection: one of the 8 M-score components (total accruals to
  total assets) is close to the "accrual anomaly," which prior runs already flagged as
  "India evidence too weak per international replication study" (2026-08-23). The
  M-score is not just accruals — it's a 7-other-ratio composite layered on top — so this
  is not a straight re-proposal of a rejected idea, but the overlap is close enough that
  I would not be surprised if a clean test shows the M-score's apparent effect is really
  just the accrual component doing the work, in which case it inherits the same
  rejection.
- No plausible confound-check against size/beta has been done anywhere I found — given
  this project's profitability-factor postmortem, that check should be a precondition,
  not an afterthought, before trusting the single India study's "lower returns" claim.

**Why it might/might not transfer to India:** For: India has had several high-profile
accounting-manipulation cases (and a tightened disclosure/audit regime post-IL&FS and
post-Satyam), so the underlying phenomenon the M-score tries to detect is not absent
here. Against: the construction-quality concern above (COGS/SG&A disclosure gaps) is
India-specific and could make the computed M-score noisy or systematically biased in a
way the single existing India study may not have controlled for.

**Data needed to test on India:** Point-in-time income-statement and balance-sheet line
items sufficient to compute all 8 Beneish ratios (receivables, COGS or a COGS proxy,
gross margin, PP&E, depreciation, SG&A, total debt, total assets, sales, total
accruals) for at least two consecutive fiscal years per firm (several ratios are
year-over-year indices). This is mostly within what the project's XBRL income-statement
collector already covers from ~2018 onward; the COGS/SG&A-split data-quality question
above should be checked against the actual XBRL tags available before building this.

**NOT YET TESTED — requires the project's permutation-test gauntlet before any
deployment consideration.**

## Investigated, not proposed this week

- **Financial distress risk anomaly (Altman Z-score / Ohlson O-score, "distress risk
  puzzle"):** well known internationally (distressed firms earn systematically lower,
  not higher, returns despite higher risk — Campbell, Hilscher & Szilagyi 2008 and
  others). Several India studies exist on distress-score *prediction accuracy*
  (bankruptcy classification), but I found no India (or other EM) study that tests
  whether distress scores predict *future stock returns*. This is a real gap in the
  literature rather than a rejection — worth a second look in a future run if a
  return-predictability study for EM/India surfaces, but there is nothing citable to
  propose on this axis today.
- **Dividend initiation/omission anomaly:** classic US result (Michaely, Thaler &
  Womack 1994), but the asymmetry shrinks once dividend-yield change is controlled for
  even in the founding paper. Emerging-market evidence found is limited to a handful of
  older, mixed-result Indonesia studies (no 2024-2025 work, no India-specific study
  found at all). Too thin and too stale to propose.
- **NCLT/IBC insolvency-filing price reaction:** India has abundant anecdotal
  single-stock examples (Jet Airways, RCom, Jaypee Infratech, BPL) of large price moves
  around insolvency-tribunal events, but I found no systematic academic event study
  measuring abnormal returns around filing/admission/dismissal/resolution dates, only
  news-anecdote coverage. Not citable as evidence yet; also likely to be a very
  low-frequency, binary/event-driven signal rather than a standing cross-sectional
  factor, which is a structural mismatch for this project's long-only entry-signal
  framework even if the underlying pattern turned out to be real.
