# Literature Scout — 2026-11-15

Two candidates this week. Both are updates to items this project's own queue had
previously marked "investigated, no citable result" rather than brand-new topics —
in both cases, deeper digging this run turned up evidence that wasn't found before.
Per the scout's rules, that counts as a genuinely new angle only because the evidentiary
basis itself is new, not because the underlying idea is unprecedented; I've said explicitly
what changed in each case.

---

## Candidate 1: Analyst earnings-estimate revision overlay (EPS change / EPS diffusion)

**What it is:** A cross-sectional signal built from the *direction and breadth of change* in
sell-side consensus EPS estimates — not the estimate level, not an earnings surprise, and not
price action. Two standard constructions: "EPS change" (the % change in consensus forward EPS
over a lookback window) and "EPS diffusion" (the net % of analysts revising estimates up minus
down, a breadth measure). Long the stocks with the most positive revision activity, short/avoid
the most negative.

**Why this is being proposed now and not closed out already:** The 2026-08-21 run investigated
"analyst estimate revisions" and recorded "no citable India-specific result found." That
conclusion no longer holds — this run found a specific, citable India result (below). The
candidate is being raised fresh on the strength of that new evidence, not because the concept
itself is new.

**Sources:**
- S&P Dow Jones Indices, "Do Earnings Revisions Matter in Asia?" (Feb 2018) —
  https://www.indexologyblog.com/2018/02/08/earnings-revision-strategies-in-asia/, full paper
  at https://www.spindices.com/documents/research/research-do-earnings-revisions-matter-in-asia.pdf
- S&P Dow Jones Indices, "Do Earnings Revisions Matter in India?" (Feb 2018) —
  https://www.indexologyblog.com/2018/02/21/do-earnings-revisions-matter-in-india/
- S&P Dow Jones Indices, "Earnings Revision Overlay on Fundamental Factors in Asia" (2018,
  covering Mar 2006–Mar 2018) — https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/research/article/earnings-revision-overlay-on-fundamental-factors-in-asia
- For historical/academic context only (not India-specific, not revisions-specific): Griffin,
  Ji & Martin (2005) on momentum across countries; a 2026 SSRN reproducibility paper, Kumar,
  "Cross-Sectional Momentum in Indian Equities" (Nifty-50, 2012–2025) —
  https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5744965 — relevant only as adjacent
  under-reaction evidence, not a direct replication of the revisions construct.

**Claimed effect and where/when documented:** The S&P DJI study backtests sector-neutral
EPS-change and EPS-diffusion strategies across seven Pan-Asian markets, 2005–2016. For India
specifically, the blog reports monthly excess returns of **0.30% (EPS change)** and **0.47%
(EPS diffusion)** over the base universe. The strategy is reported to work in Korea, India and
Taiwan but explicitly *not* in Japan. A companion India-specific blog post states that, for
India, "size bias was not an important driver of excess returns" — a direct (if informal) claim
that this is not simply another small-cap proxy.

**Mechanism:** Analysts under-react to new information and revise estimates gradually rather
than immediately (well-documented analyst anchoring/conservatism bias), so a current revision
signals more revisions are still coming — a behavioral under-reaction story, structurally the
same family as price/earnings momentum and PEAD, just measured off the sell-side estimate
series instead of price or the earnings-announcement surprise itself.

**Evidence-strength assessment — my own read, separate from what S&P claims:**
- This is **vendor practitioner research (S&P Dow Jones Indices), not a peer-reviewed academic
  paper.** S&P sells factor indices and licenses strategies; it has a direct commercial interest
  in finding tradeable factors. That doesn't make the numbers wrong, but it's a materially
  weaker evidentiary tier than an academic journal study, and I could not independently verify
  the backtest methodology (point-in-time-ness of the consensus data, survivorship handling,
  cost assumptions) — I only have the blog-level summary, not the underlying workpapers.
- The paper itself flags **high turnover**, comparable to momentum strategies — meaning returns
  net of realistic transaction costs and taxes could look very different from the gross numbers
  quoted, and this project has not estimated those costs.
- There is an internal **inconsistency across S&P's own blog posts** worth flagging rather than
  papering over: the Pan-Asia-wide framing says alpha is generally larger in small-caps than
  large/mid-caps, while a separate India-specific post claims size bias was *not* important for
  India. I could not resolve this from the material available (no full paper access) — it needs
  resolving before any confidence is placed on the "it's not a size proxy" claim, especially
  given this project's own profitability-factor experience of a signal collapsing once properly
  controlled for size and beta.
- The construct sits **mechanistically adjacent to PEAD**, which this project deprioritized
  because India-relevant work found it ≈ zero net of costs in liquid names (Chordia et al.).
  Revisions and announcement-drift are not the same signal, but they draw on the same
  under-reaction phenomenon, so a prior toward "decays once costs and liquidity are handled
  properly" is reasonable here too.

**Why it might or might not transfer to India:** It already has a direct, India-specific
backtest (unlike most candidates in this queue), which is unusual and worth something. But that
backtest is 2005–2016 — a decade stale — on a vendor's own index-construction universe, not
necessarily this project's actual tradable universe, and says nothing about whether the effect
has since been arbitraged away (plausible, given sell-side estimate data is now cheap and
widely distributed via terminals).

**Data needed:** A consensus EPS estimate time series per stock, ideally with revision history
(not just current consensus) — e.g., Refinitiv I/B/E/S, Bloomberg BEst, or a cheaper India-local
source if one has usable point-in-time history (unverified; this project has not checked). This
is a genuinely new data type for the project (sell-side consensus estimates), distinct from the
XBRL actuals collector.

## Candidate 2: Working-capital-efficiency / trade-credit threshold effect on stock returns

**What it is:** A signal built from the cash conversion cycle (CCC = days inventory + days
receivable − days payable) or more granular trade-credit extended/received, tested directly
against *future stock returns/alpha* — not profitability, not margins.

**Why this is being proposed now and not closed out already:** The 2026-11-01 run recorded
working-capital days/cash-conversion-cycle as investigated-not-proposed with the explicit
reason "only profitability-correlation studies, no return test." That gap is what changed —
this run found an India-specific study that does test returns, not just profitability.

**Sources:**
- Banerjee, Kundu & Sivasankaran, "Asymmetric Impact of Working Capital Efficiency on Market
  Performance of Indian Firms," *Global Business Review* 25(3), 2024, pp. 705–723
  (DOI 10.1177/0972150920988648) — https://ideas.repec.org/a/sae/globus/v25y2024i3p705-723.html
- Hill, Kelly & Lockhart, "Downstream Value of Upstream Finance," *Financial Review* 48(4), 2013,
  pp. 697–723 — https://aquila.usm.edu/fac_pubs/20293
- Hill & Kelly, "Equity Returns and the Supply of Trade Credit," *Financial Management* 41(1),
  2012, pp. 255–280 — https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:finmgt:v:41:y:2012:i:1:p:255-280
- Hill, Kelly & Venkiteshwaran, *Journal of Financial Research* 38(3), 2015, pp. 305–317 (lower
  market value of receivables for firms with higher lagged receivables) —
  https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfnres:v:38:y:2015:i:3:p:305-317
- Hirshleifer, Li, Lourie & Ruchti, NBER Working Paper 25553 (supplier private information,
  payment timeliness predicts future stock returns) — https://www.nber.org/papers/w25553
- Context, not return evidence: Altaf, "Stock liquidity and trade credit: Evidence from Indian
  firms," *International Journal of Finance & Economics* 27(3), 2022, pp. 3475–3483; Baker,
  Pattnaik & Kumar (2022), BSE-listed firms, inverted-U relationship between trade credit and
  profitability (not returns).

**Claimed effect and where/when documented:** The India-specific result (Banerjee, Kundu &
Sivasankaran 2024) uses GMM on 718 BSE-listed non-financial firms across 11 industries,
2011–2017, and finds a **threshold/asymmetric effect**: CCC above a threshold level is
associated with significantly lower excess stock returns (alpha); below the threshold, no
significant effect. Neither a simple quadratic specification nor industry-median-relative CCC
reproduces the result — only the threshold framing does, per the authors. The separate US
literature (Hill & Kelly and co-authors, three papers across three journals, 2012–2015) finds a
related but distinct "counterparty financing" story: firms extending more trade credit to
customers show a market-value/return relationship with that trade credit exposure, with
cross-sectional variation by product liquidity and competition.

**Mechanism:** Two overlapping but not identical stories. (1) Real economic friction: trade
credit is implicit financing a firm extends to customers or receives from suppliers; firms that
over-extend relative to an efficient threshold tie up capital and bear customer-default risk,
which the market prices as higher expected risk/lower subsequent return once the imbalance
exceeds a workable threshold. (2) Information-based: suppliers may hold private information
about buyer customers through the payment relationship (Hirshleifer et al.), so payment-
timeliness/trade-credit variables carry information the market under-reacts to.

**Evidence-strength assessment — my own read:**
- This is **better-evidenced than most candidates on this queue on the mechanism side**: three
  distinct peer-reviewed US papers (Financial Management, Financial Review, Journal of Financial
  Research) from overlapping authors, i.e. a real multi-paper lineage, not a single study — but
  it is the *same small group of authors* building out one research agenda, which is weaker than
  independent replication by unrelated teams.
- I could not pin down one clean, independently-confirmed headline effect size for the US
  "counterparty factor" from the secondary sources available to me this run (a Kenan Institute
  summary cites a ~0.6%/month, ~7.6%/year spread attributed to a "counterparty factor," but I
  could not confirm which paper that number traces to, so **I am not reporting that figure as
  verified** — flagging the gap rather than passing an unconfirmed number through as fact).
  There is also a separately-titled piece, "On the Diminishing Return to Trade Credit," which by
  its title alone suggests the relationship is non-monotonic/weakens at higher levels — consistent
  with, but not identical to, the Indian paper's own threshold (non-linear) framing.
- The **one India-specific result (Banerjee et al. 2024) is a single study**, sample ends 2017
  (stale, pre-dates this project's own 2018-onward XBRL coverage window), and uses a composite
  CCC measure rather than receivables and payables separately — so it doesn't yet tell us
  whether the effect is driven by the receivables side, the payables side, or both.
- **Confound risk, flagged explicitly per this project's own profitability-factor postmortem:**
  CCC and trade-credit intensity both vary systematically with sector (capital goods, auto
  components and infra/EPC carry structurally different receivable/payable profiles than FMCG
  or IT services) and with firm size/bargaining power (larger firms can push payment terms onto
  smaller suppliers). Any India test of this would need explicit sector and size/beta controls
  from the start, not added after a promising raw result the way profitability was.

**Why it might or might not transfer to India:** The one direct India test found a return
effect, which is more than most candidates in this queue can claim going in. But the evidentiary
base is thin (one study, dated sample, composite rather than decomposed CCC), and India's trade
credit institutional backdrop — a market with historically weak legal enforcement of payment
terms and the 2023 MSME 45-day payment mandate under Section 43B(h) of the Income Tax Act
changing incentives for large buyers — may make the relationship behave differently going
forward than it did in the 2011–2017 sample.

**Data needed:** Trade receivables, trade payables and inventory — all balance-sheet line
items. This project's own XBRL collector only has balance-sheet history from roughly 2023
onward, the same constraint already parking the asset-growth, inventory-growth and
capacity-overhang candidates on this queue as "pending data extension." This candidate is
**data-blocked today** for the same reason — it should be grouped with those three on the
backlog rather than treated as independently ready to test.

**Also investigated this run, not proposed:**
- *CEO/management succession and generational transition in Indian family-controlled business
  groups* — a genuinely new data type (leadership-transition events), and India has real
  academic interest in family-business succession as a governance topic. But I could not find a
  rigorous India-specific event study with actual abnormal-return figures — only a 1992–2006
  ResearchGate paper on succession-announcement wealth effects whose return results I could not
  access, plus family-firm CEO-transition event studies from France/Germany/UK and Malaysia (not
  India), and India-specific evidence that is only press/brokerage commentary on individual stock
  moves (Jindal Steel, Godrej Consumer, Kotak Mahindra, Bata, Asian Paints), not a controlled
  study. Even where this effect is documented elsewhere, it's a short-window announcement
  reaction, the same decay-prone shape already flagged for several other closed-door-adjacent
  candidates on this queue (PEAD, buyback drift, index-reconstitution pop).
- *Order backlog / order-book disclosure mispricing* (Rajgopal, Shevlin & Venkatachalam 2003,
  *Review of Accounting Studies*, and later work) — strong, real academic pedigree (a hedge
  strategy exploiting backlog mispricing reported at 7.7% abnormal return in the working-paper
  version, robust to Fama-French controls, incremental to the accrual anomaly) and a genuinely
  new data type. Not proposed because (a) US backlog disclosure is an SEC-norm quarterly
  practice; Indian capital-goods/EPC/infra firms disclose order books voluntarily and
  inconsistently via investor presentations and press releases, not a structured filing field,
  so this would need sector-by-sector manual curation comparable in effort to the
  customer-momentum candidate already flagged (2026-09-20), and (b) mechanistically it's in the
  same sell-side-under-reaction-to-future-earnings-information family as this week's Candidate 1
  and the already-deprioritized PEAD, so it isn't fully independent evidence of a new
  phenomenon.
- *Debt-issuance anomaly* (firms that issue or retire more debt than their asset-growth rate
  implies earn lower subsequent returns) — real academic evidence, but overlaps heavily with the
  already-queued net-share-issuance candidate (2026-10-11) as the same "financing-activity
  anomaly" family, and India's corporate bond market is dominated by illiquid private placements
  with no clean bulk primary-issuance data source identified, so feasibility looks worse than
  the equity-issuance variant already on the queue.
- Re-checked briefly and found nothing new: FII/DII flow data, promoter pledge data availability
  (status unchanged from the 2026-08-23 follow-up).
