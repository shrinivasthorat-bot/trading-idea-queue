# Literature Scout — 2026-10-25

Two candidates this week, both genuinely new data types relative to everything in
INDEX.md/REJECTED.md to date. Checked both against the existing queue before writing this up;
neither overlaps with the open asset-growth (balance-sheet-blocked), accrual, or any
alt-data candidate already on file.

---

## Candidate 1: Abnormal Inventory Growth (operations-based investment anomaly)

**What it is:** A firm's inventory growth in excess of what its sales growth would justify
("abnormal inventory growth," AIG) as a *negative* predictor of future stock returns — firms
that overbuild inventory relative to demand underperform afterward.

**Sources:**
- Kesavan, S. & Mani, V. (2013), "The Relationship Between Abnormal Inventory Growth and
  Future Earnings for U.S. Public Retailers," *Manufacturing & Service Operations Management*
  15(1). https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/msom.1120.0389 — original study, U.S.
  retailers, inverted-U relationship between AIG and one-year-ahead EPS, and reports an
  AIG-sorted strategy earns significant abnormal stock returns.
- Pitari, N. (2020), replication of the Kesavan-Mani model — found the AIG→earnings link
  *not* statistically significant for 2004-2009 but significant (and inverted-U shaped) for
  2013-2018, i.e. the result is period-dependent in its own home market.
- **Aziz, T. & Sabir, L.B. (2022), "Do inventory dynamics predict stock returns? An
  empirical examination of Indian manufacturing firms," *International Journal of Services
  and Operations Management* 42(3), 397-421 (Inderscience).** https://inderscience.com/offers.php?id=124273
  — this is the India-specific study: higher abnormal inventory growth → lower future stock
  returns, concentrated in automobile, textile, agricultural-products and plastics sectors;
  inventory *turnover* alone has only a weak link to future returns (consistent with that
  being already priced in). I could only access the abstract — full methodology,
  sample size and robustness checks are unverified.
- A Tehran Stock Exchange study (2008-2013, 109 firms) is also cited in secondary sources as
  finding a "significant and direct relationship between abnormal inventory growth and
  long-term stock returns" — **note this is the opposite sign** from the U.S. and India
  findings (positive vs. negative). I was not able to pull the original Tehran paper to check
  if it's measuring the same construct or a different one; flagging this explicitly rather
  than quietly picking the India-consistent studies and ignoring the contradiction.

**My assessment of the mechanism:** The dominant story (Kesavan-Mani, and in the same family
as Thomas & Zhang's investment-anomaly logic already on this project's queue via the
2026-10-04 asset-growth candidate) is overproduction/demand-misjudgment: excess inventory
build predicts markdown risk and margin compression that the market doesn't immediately
price in. That's a plausible, not-purely-statistical mechanism. But the sign-reversal in the
Tehran result, plus the period-dependence found even in the U.S. replication, means this is
not a clean, settled effect — it's evidence worth testing, not evidence that's already
converged. This is exactly the kind of "could this be a confound/era-specific artifact"
question the profitability-factor postmortem taught this project to ask up front, not after
a negative result.

**Why it might or might not transfer to India:** It's a genuinely new line-item-level data
type (inventory + COGS dynamics, not top-line balance-sheet totals), and inventory-heavy
sectors (auto components, textiles, FMCG manufacturing) are a meaningful slice of the NSE
universe, so this isn't obviously a narrow-applicability idea. Risks: (1) India's working-
capital financing costs and credit cycle differ from the U.S./Iran, which could change the
overinvestment penalty's size or even sign; (2) the one India study found is sector-specific
in its significant results, not universal, so a naive cross-sectional implementation might
dilute the effect; (3) it sits in the same conceptual family as the already-queued asset-
growth candidate, so if both get tested they should be checked for shared variance rather
than treated as fully independent bets.

**Data needed / obtainability:** Inventory (from the balance sheet) and COGS/sales (from the
income statement), used to build a sales-growth-adjusted "abnormal" inventory measure, not
just raw inventory YoY — and ideally several years of history per firm to avoid exactly the
period-dependence problem found in the U.S. replication. **This is data-blocked today**: the
project's XBRL collector only has balance-sheet history from ~2023, the same constraint that
already parked the 2026-10-04 asset-growth candidate as "pending data extension." This
candidate should probably be evaluated together with that one once balance-sheet history is
deep enough, not tested in isolation first.

**NOT YET TESTED — requires the project's permutation-test gauntlet before any deployment
consideration.**

## Candidate 2: Online job-posting intensity ("hiring-intent") signal

**What it is:** Growth in a firm's online job postings — read as a forward-looking,
voluntary disclosure of expansion plans, issued outside the normal investor-relations
channel — as a *positive* predictor of future sales/profit growth and (in the U.S.) future
stock returns.

**Sources:**
- Gutiérrez, E., Lourie, B., Nekrasov, A. & Shevlin, T. (2020), "Are Online Job Postings
  Informative to Investors?," *Management Science* 66(7), 3133-3141 (SSRN version 2019).
  https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2019.3450 — top-tier journal. Finds growth
  in job postings tracks later operating performance, with a stronger relationship when
  postings look like growth-hiring rather than backfill, and a positive short-window (2-day)
  price reaction when postings increase, concentrated in growth-type and low-labor-intensity
  firms.
- Lev, B. & Wu, X. (2022), "Identifying Overvalued Stocks with Corporate Job Postings," SSRN
  4112800 (working paper, not yet confirmed as peer-reviewed-published). U.S. data
  (Burning Glass), 2007-2017, top price-to-book tercile only. Finds posting growth
  distinguishes richly-but-fairly-valued growth stocks from overvalued ones, and predicts
  sales/gross-profit growth 3+ years out net of standard growth predictors.
  https://www.ivey.uwo.ca/media/h01llll0/identifying-overvalued-stocks-with-corporate-job-postings.pdf
- A Taiwanese web-crawled-vacancies study (reported in *Pacific-Basin Finance Journal*, 2020)
  is cited as a non-U.S. replication finding job openings foreshadow operating performance
  both via a stock-price channel and directly — I could not pull the exact author/title/DOI
  to verify this independently, so treat it as a lead to confirm, not a confirmed citation.
- A vendor-sourced (not independently verified) claim about a ~100-million-ad U.S.
  energy-sector study should be discounted; I'm not citing it as evidence.

**My assessment of the mechanism:** Plausible and distinct from every attention/disclosure
mechanism already investigated by this scout (media coverage, Google Trends, analyst
coverage, earnings-call text) — this is a *firm-generated, operational* leading indicator
(what the firm is actually doing) rather than third-party attention or sentiment. The core
Management Science paper is the single best-pedigreed piece of evidence surfaced in any
scout run so far. That said, every paper I found is built on U.S. online-labor-market data
(Burning Glass / similar); I found **zero India-specific evidence either way**, so this is a
real literature gap, not a confirmed EM transfer.

**Confound risk, explicitly:** This project's profitability-factor postmortem showed a
fundamentals-sounding effect can collapse into a size proxy. Raw job-posting volume almost
certainly scales with firm size, and India adds a channel-coverage problem the U.S. samples
don't have as acutely: formal online job portals (Naukri, LinkedIn, Indeed India) skew toward
white-collar/IT-services hiring, while blue-collar and contract-labour hiring — a large share
of Indian manufacturing employment — often goes through placement agencies, union channels,
or word-of-mouth and would be invisible to an online-postings feed. A naive implementation
would likely proxy for "IT/services, large-cap" rather than capturing genuine hiring-intent
signal across the full NSE universe. Any test needs to normalize for firm size/sector from
the start, not discover the confound after a positive-looking backtest.

**Why it might or might not transfer to India:** The underlying economic logic (markets
under-react to a diffuse, non-IR-channel data source) doesn't depend on being in the U.S., so
there's no obvious reason it couldn't work here — but the channel-coverage issue above means
the *construction* would need real rethinking, not a direct port of the U.S. methodology.

**Data needed / obtainability — the binding constraint:** This is not in the project's
existing XBRL collector at all; it would be a wholly new alternative-data pipeline. Worse,
it's not just a paywall problem like the pending Trendlyne/GuruQ data (pledge, estimates):
job postings get taken down once filled, so there is **no public backward-looking bulk
archive** to buy or scrape retroactively. Naukri has no public API. One vendor (TheirStack)
publishes an India job-postings series starting only around November 2022, and I could not
confirm its per-posting history depth or pricing. Realistically, testing this candidate means
either paying for a vendor's existing India history (if one proves deep and clean enough —
unverified) or standing up a scraper now and waiting to accumulate a usable multi-year
sample prospectively. That is a materially longer lead time than any other data-availability
gap flagged in this project's queue so far, and should be weighed against that before
committing effort.

**NOT YET TESTED — requires the project's permutation-test gauntlet before any deployment
consideration.**

## Investigated and not proposed this week

- **Website/app-traffic "digital footprint" data** (e.g., Konchitchki et al., *The Accounting
  Review*, 2026, SimilarWeb data; a Stanford GSB working paper; Chen, Liu & Wu 2024 on app
  downloads) — multiple well-regarded U.S. studies, but zero emerging-market replication
  found, and the same no-backward-archive / paid-vendor problem as the job-postings
  candidate above, without even job-postings' one non-U.S. (Taiwan) data point. Parked as
  weaker than Candidate 2, not independently proposed.
- **Monsoon rainfall as a rural-consumption/FMCG return predictor** — a genuinely India-
  specific, often-repeated market narrative, but the one methodologically careful recent
  study found (2026, five NSE sector indices, OLS/CAPM/ARIMA/GARCH/regime-switching) reports
  inconsistent and limited direct effects; the supportive literature is older and more
  narrative than quantitative. Not strong enough evidence to propose.
- **Auditor resignation / going-concern qualification as a return predictor** — real India
  anecdotes (Manpasand Beverages, Vakrangee, Jet Airways) and a SEBI disclosure regime
  (Oct-2019 circular) exist, but no academic study testing forward return predictability was
  found, only press coverage of individual price drops around the resignation announcement
  itself — the same pre-disclosure/event-window problem that already weakened several earlier
  candidates (promoter transactions, bulk/block deals, SEBI enforcement actions).
- **Independent director resignation as a governance red flag** — one rigorous study found
  (Chakrabarti, Subramanian & Tung, using the post-Satyam resignation wave) reports a ~-1.3%
  four-day announcement CAR, stronger for audit-committee members — but this is an
  announcement-event effect, not evidence of a standing, tradeable cross-sectional factor,
  and the sample is tied to one historical crisis episode.
- **Mutual fund "active share" / crowded-trade signal** — no India study found that tests
  active share or crowding directly against forward returns; the India mutual-fund-skill
  literature located (an IIM Bangalore dissertation, older stock-picking-ability studies) is
  about fund performance, not a stock-level signal, and doesn't address crowding at all.

Also re-confirmed before writing this up: nothing in REJECTED.md or INDEX.md's 2026-08-21
through 2026-10-18 entries covers inventory dynamics or any digital/hiring alt-data type, so
both candidates above are genuinely new ground, not re-proposals.
