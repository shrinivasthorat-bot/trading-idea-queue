## 1. Capacity overhang (real-options excess-capacity measure)

**Source(s):**
- Aretz, K. & Pope, P. (2018), "Real Options Models of the Firm, Capacity Overhang, and the
  Cross-Section of Stock Returns," *Journal of Finance*, 73(3), 1363–1415.
  https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v73y2018i3p1363-1415.html
  (working paper: https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/85963)
- Aretz, K. & Kagkadis, A. (2025), "Construction, Real Uncertainty, and Stock-Level
  Investment Anomalies," *Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis* (forthcoming/2025).
  https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/8B794D8AC51CDAC0CA06C562DC44052F/S0022109024000024a.pdf/construction-real-uncertainty-and-stock-level-investment-anomalies.pdf

**Claimed effect / markets / periods:** Aretz & Pope build a stochastic-frontier estimate of
"capacity overhang" — installed production capacity minus the firm's ex-ante *optimal*
capacity given its demand and cost structure — and find it has a significant *negative*
relation to the cross-section of U.S. stock returns, surviving the standard factor controls,
and robust across small/large and more/less reversible-investment subsamples. They argue it
helps explain *why* momentum and profitability anomalies exist (firms that already have
overhang are less sensitive to further demand shocks), but does not explain the value or
investment anomalies. The 2025 follow-up (Aretz & Kagkadis) narrows in on the investment
anomaly specifically and reports that the well-known negative relation between real
investment and future returns is concentrated in the subsample of firms that are *building
new capacity* (as opposed to e.g. replacing worn-out assets or M&A), which they attribute to
elevated idiosyncratic production-cost uncertainty during the "learning" period after new
capacity comes online. All evidence found is U.S.-sample; I found no emerging-market or
India replication of either paper.

**Mechanism:** Economic, not purely statistical. Two linked channels: (a) a real-options
channel — firms with spare capacity hold valuable-but-unexercised growth options, which
are priced like options (lower realized risk premium than the underlying asset) — a
structural/risk-based story, not a behavioral one; (b) a learning-curve channel — newly
built capacity carries unresolved production-cost uncertainty that gets priced down until
the firm learns to run it efficiently. Both are plausible and have some theoretical grounding
in corporate-finance real-options literature predating these two papers.

**Evidence strength:** Single research group (same two authors link the two papers), but
published in a top-tier outlet (JF) with a JFQA follow-up — stronger single-study pedigree
than most candidates this project has looked at (comparable to the inventory-growth or
job-posting candidates' single-paper pedigree, but in a higher-ranked journal). No
out-of-US-sample replication found despite a dedicated search — this is a real gap, not
just something I failed to find in five minutes; it means the result's generalizability is
unproven, not just untested in India specifically.

**Why distinct from this project's queue:** The already-queued balance-sheet asset-growth
candidate (flagged 2026-10-04, still data-blocked) uses *total* asset growth — an
accounting-based investment measure with no view on whether the firm is over- or
under-capacity relative to its own demand. Capacity overhang is a structural/model-based
construct (installed vs. optimal capacity, estimated via a production frontier), built from
physical/operating capacity data, not accounting growth rates — a different claim about a
different confound (real-options mispricing / learning-curve uncertainty, not a size proxy).
It is also distinct from the already-rejected profitability and already-queued abnormal
inventory-growth candidates, which concern balance-sheet stocks and flows, not a frontier
estimate of capacity relative to demand.

**India transfer and confounds:** No priors either way, since there is no non-U.S. test. The
project's own profitability-factor postmortem is the obvious thing to check for first: does
"low capacity overhang" (i.e., tightly-utilized, high-conviction expansion) just relabel
large-cap/high-quality exposure, the mirror image of the small-cap confound that killed
profitability? This needs an explicit size/beta/quality control before trusting any result,
exactly as flagged repeatedly elsewhere in this queue.

**Data needed and availability:** This is the binding constraint. Estimating a capacity
frontier credibly needs a reasonably long panel (plausibly 10+ years) of revenue and
fixed-asset/PP&E (or physical capacity-utilization) data per firm to fit the stochastic
frontier model Pope's companion methodology paper uses. The project's XBRL collector only
has balance-sheet history from ~2023 — nowhere near enough. This sits on the queue as
**pending data extension**, the same status already given to the asset-growth and
inventory-growth candidates. Even a cruder proxy (e.g., gross PP&E growth relative to
trailing revenue growth, without the full frontier estimation) would still need several years
of balance-sheet history this project doesn't yet have.

**NOT YET TESTED — requires the project's permutation-test gauntlet before any deployment
consideration.**

## 2. Key Audit Matters (KAM) disclosure content as an underreaction/risk signal

**Source(s):**
- Review: "The consequences of disclosing key audit matters (KAMs): A review of the academic
  literature," 2019/2020 (Zenodo preprint). https://zenodo.org/records/2597742
- Kenya: Strathmore University thesis, "Disclosure of key audit matters and market reaction:
  the case of companies listed on the Nairobi Securities Exchange."
  https://su-plus.strathmore.edu/handle/11071/12518
- China: "Key audit matters and stock price synchronicity: Evidence from a quasi-natural
  experiment in China" (UTS repository). https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/handle/10453/152428
- Egypt: "The effect of key audit matters disclosure on stock prices and trading [volume]"
  (PSAU repository). https://psau.sa.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/the-effect-of-key-audit-matters-disclosure-on-stock-prices-and-tr
- Canada and Malaysia findings (S&P/TSX event study 2020–2022; Bursa Malaysia "KAMSCORE"
  study 2016–2019) are referenced only via a search-engine summary with no primary-source
  URL I could independently pin down — **flagged as unverified secondary claims, not
  confirmed against the original papers.**

**Claimed effect / markets / periods:** SA 701 / ISA 701-style rules require auditors of
listed companies to disclose the specific "key audit matters" they focused on (e.g.,
impairment testing, revenue recognition, valuation of financial instruments, going concern)
rather than a boilerplate opinion. Across the markets found — Kenya, China, and Egypt
directly verified, Canada and Malaysia only via unverified summary — studies report
statistically significant stock-price/volume reactions tied to KAM disclosure, and the China
study specifically finds KAM disclosure reduces stock-price synchronicity (i.e., it conveys
firm-specific information the market didn't already have). Most tellingly, the Kenya thesis
reports the *cumulative abnormal return* effect *rises over the first year* after disclosure
before fading — i.e., not purely a same-day price reaction, which would be the normal
"announcement already priced in" pattern. If that specific finding replicates, it implies
slow diffusion/underreaction to KAM content, not instant arbitrage.

**Mechanism:** Plausible and falls into a mechanism family already treated as real by this
project (limited-attention/information-diffusion, the same broad category behind PEAD and
the media-attention candidate) — but applied to an audit disclosure that is denser,
less-followed, and arguably harder for retail/even institutional investors to parse than a
headline earnings number, which is exactly the kind of setting where underreaction stories
are most credible.

**Evidence strength:** Multi-market (3 directly verified, 2 more only as unverified claims),
but every study found is a market-reaction/event-study design measuring returns in a window
around the KAM disclosure date (mostly the annual-report filing date) — not a true
cross-sectional forward-return backtest of "buy low-KAM-risk firms, short high-KAM-risk
firms" sustained over a holding period. The Kenya paper's one-year drift finding is the only
hint of something beyond a pure announcement effect, and it's a single unpublished thesis,
not a peer-reviewed journal result. I could not verify the Canada/Malaysia claims against
primary sources, so treat the "multi-market" characterization as partly unconfirmed.

**Why distinct from this project's queue:** This is a different data type from everything
already investigated: not price/volume (closed door), not ownership/flow data (FII/DII,
promoter, mutual-fund breadth), not accounting ratios (profitability, accruals, Beneish,
asset/inventory growth), not corporate-action announcements (buybacks, reconstitution,
dividends). It is the *structured content of the audit report itself* — distinct even from
the already-investigated "Fog index/annual-report readability" (2026-09-13, which measured
prose complexity of the whole annual report and was found to predict *synchronicity*, not
returns) and "auditor resignation/going-concern qualifications" (2026-10-25, which looked at
a binary resignation/qualification event, not the granular list of flagged risk areas every
listed company's audit report now contains). No prior run has looked at KAM.

**India transfer and confounds:** SA 701 (the Indian standard, aligned with ISA 701) became
mandatory for listed-entity audits for periods beginning on or after 1 April 2018 — meaning
KAM data has existed in India for roughly the same window the project's XBRL income-statement
collector already covers (~2018 onward), which is a real practical advantage over several
other candidates in this queue. The obvious confound to check first: more KAMs, or
higher-risk KAM topics (impairment, going concern, related-party transactions), very plausibly
just proxy for larger/more complex firms with bigger, messier balance sheets — a different
flavor of the same "factor is secretly a size/complexity proxy" problem that killed
profitability. This would need an explicit control for firm size and audit-fee/complexity
before any result could be trusted.

**Data needed and availability:** KAM paragraphs are disclosed in the standalone audit
report, which is filed alongside annual reports (and often within the same XBRL/annual-report
filing package already in scope for this project's collector) — but as free text, not a
structured numeric field, so testing this requires text extraction/classification (count of
KAMs, and/or classification by topic — impairment vs. going-concern vs. revenue recognition
vs. other) rather than a simple field pull. This is a non-trivial but plausibly tractable
NLP/parsing task on top of filings the collector likely already ingests, which is a lower
data-acquisition bar than most of the alt-data candidates already queued (no paywall, no
new data vendor, no from-scratch scrape).

**NOT YET TESTED — requires the project's permutation-test gauntlet before any deployment
consideration.**

## Investigated, not proposed

- **Mutual-fund proxy-voting dissent** (SEBI-mandated AMC voting disclosures on AGM
  resolutions) — real, India-specific, and genuinely new data type, but I could not find any
  study, academic or otherwise, that links dissent votes to *subsequent stock returns*; the
  only India-specific research found (IIM Kozhikode, IIM Bangalore) addresses voting
  *patterns* and conflicts of interest, not a return effect. No citable evidence base to
  propose a factor from.
- **LIC/insurance-company stake changes as a "smart money" signal** — plenty of news
  coverage of LIC buying/selling specific stocks, but no systematic study, and the one
  aggregate comparison I found (stocks LIC added to outperforming stocks it trimmed) is a
  simple one-period average from a news article, not a controlled test. Also overlaps
  heavily with the already-investigated aggregate institutional-ownership-change candidate
  (2026-08-30), which found contradictory/weak evidence.
- **NSE/BSE delivery-percentage (deliverable volume) as a "genuine investment vs.
  speculation" signal** — distinct data field from pure price/volume OHLC, but (a) the only
  backtest found is an unreviewed newsletter series showing the strategy *underperforms* the
  index once restricted to a liquid Nifty-50 universe, and (b) this sits uncomfortably close
  to the already-closed-door technical/volume-indicator space (OBV and volume were part of
  the 11 indicators killed in the original technical-signal test). Not proposed.
- **Government/infrastructure contract-win announcements** as a capital-goods/infra entry
  signal — the one academic event study found reports the average abnormal return around
  contract-win announcements is *not* statistically significant, despite abundant bullish
  trade-press anecdotes. Thin and contradicted by the only rigorous source found.
- **Management revenue/margin guidance revisions** (IT-sector practice of issuing forward
  guidance) — no systematic academic study found at all, only news-article case examples
  (Infosys, TCS, Accenture-driven sector moves). Not enough evidence base to propose.
- **Auditor Big-4/non-Big-4 switching** as a quality/risk signal — mixed and mostly
  statistically insignificant return evidence across the Asian-market studies found; one
  Indonesia study finds an effect, a Hong Kong/China study finds an effect only for
  non-Big4-to-non-Big4 switches, others find nothing. Too inconsistent to propose.
- **Working-capital days / cash-conversion-cycle** as a standalone factor — found only
  profitability-correlation studies (not return-predictability), several over a decade old.
  No asset-pricing test found.
- **Import/export customs-data (ICEGATE/DGFT shipment-level records)** as a leading revenue
  indicator — a genuinely new, India-obtainable public data type, but I found zero
  academic return-predictability evidence for any market, only data-vendor marketing claims.
  Weaker evidentiary basis than even the already-queued job-posting candidate (which at
  least has a *Management Science* paper behind it) — flagged as a from-scratch-evidence
  idea, not proposed this week.
- **Single-name credit spreads / CDS term-structure slope** (Han & Subrahmanyam-style
  信号) — good U.S. evidence and a clean limited-attention mechanism, but ruled out on data
  grounds before even reaching the evidence-quality question: India has essentially no
  liquid single-name corporate CDS market for domestically listed companies, so there is no
  realistic way to construct this signal for an India cross-section today.
- **Post-spin-off/demerger long-run abnormal-return drift** (distinct from the short-window
  announcement pop already well-documented in India) — the foundational U.S. result
  (Cusatis-Miles-Woolridge 1993) is directly disputed by its own follow-on literature
  (McConnell-Ozbilgin-Wahal 2001 call a tradable replication "fraught with ambiguities"),
  and the most recent evidence found (two 2021 European studies) *disagrees with each other*
  on whether the long-run drift exists at all outside the U.S. Every India-specific study
  found is a short announcement-window event study, the same pattern already flagged
  repeatedly in this queue (PEAD, buybacks, index reconstitution) as a sign the effect has
  likely decayed to an instantly-arbitraged announcement pop rather than a standing factor.
  Not proposed.
