## Candidate 1: Customer-supplier economic links ("customer momentum")

**Description:** A supplier firm's stock return next month is predicted by its major
customer's stock return this month. The market underreacts to news that hits a firm's
customer, so the resulting price move only partially and slowly propagates to economically
linked suppliers — creating a tradeable lag.

**Sources:**
- Cohen, L., & Frazzini, A. (2008), "Economic Links and Predictable Returns," *Journal of
  Finance*. Using US customer-supplier relationships disclosed under SFAS 131 (1980-2004), a
  strategy long suppliers of good-news customers / short suppliers of bad-news customers earns
  ~1.45-1.55%/month (t-stat ~3.6-5.4, roughly 18%/yr), robust to the three-factor model,
  liquidity, and firm/industry momentum controls.
- Chinese A-share replication (2011-2019, using supply-chain network data disclosed by listed
  companies): customer stock returns significantly positively predict supplier returns the
  following month, and the effect strengthens with supply-chain "network centrality" (how
  connected the customer is).
- Pinchuk, M. (2018/2023 update), "Customer Momentum" (arXiv:2301.11394) — extends and
  re-confirms the effect is present across multiple past-return window widths, not just the
  original specification.
- Yamamoto, Kawadai & Miyahara (FactSet research note) — extend the strategy to a global
  supply-chain network using suppliers' revenue-exposure weights to each customer as a
  weighting scheme, reporting the effect generalizes outside the US.

**Mechanism:** Limited investor attention. Most investors and analysts covering a supplier
company do not fully track news at that firm's customers, especially when the customer is a
large, widely-covered firm and the supplier is smaller and less followed. Information about
demand shocks at the customer therefore diffuses into the supplier's price with a lag rather
than instantly, exactly the same underreaction-to-indirect-information logic that motivates
PEAD (already deprioritized in this project) and industry/lead-lag momentum — but propagated
through a *production network* link rather than through the stock's own earnings or an
industry classification. This is a real, well-articulated behavioral-finance story, not a
pattern with no rationale.

**Evidence strength:** Good — genuine multi-market replication (US founding result, China
replication, a global extension), a large and statistically strong effect size in the original
study, and a mechanism that has survived several robustness checks (own-firm momentum,
industry momentum, lead-lag effects controlled for separately). This is one of the more
heavily replicated candidates this queue has surfaced, closer in evidentiary weight to the
international asset-growth/value literature than to a single-study finding.

**Confound risk (explicit, given this project's own history):** Suppliers with the largest
attention-based lag are very likely to skew toward smaller, less-covered, less-liquid firms —
structurally similar to the segment whose "profitability premium" collapsed into a size/beta
proxy once controlled. Any India test of this needs the same explicit size and liquidity
controls used in the profitability postmortem before trusting a raw spread. It is also worth
checking whether the effect is just a slower-diffusing version of already-rejected industry/
sign-consistency momentum patterns (item 3 in REJECTED.md) — the underlying propagation
mechanism is adjacent, even though the specific linkage (named customer-supplier pairs, not a
generic industry bucket or the stock's own price history) is a genuinely different construct
and data type.

**Why it might transfer to India:** India's analyst-coverage universe is even more
concentrated in large/liquid names than the US, so the attention gap between a well-covered
large customer (e.g., an auto OEM, a large pharma company, a PSU) and a smaller, thinly-covered
ancillary or component supplier is plausibly *wider* than in the US sample Cohen-Frazzini used
— which, if the mechanism is real, should make the effect stronger here, not weaker.

**Why it might not:** India's segment-reporting disclosure (Ind AS 108, paragraph 34) requires
disclosing that revenue from a single customer exceeds 10% of the total *and the amount*, but
explicitly does **not** require disclosing the customer's identity. This is a materially
different disclosure regime from the US, where SFAS 131 filings frequently do name the
customer (sometimes directly, sometimes inferable from context), which is exactly the raw
input Cohen-Frazzini's replications used. Without an equivalent named-customer feed, the core
data this factor needs does not fall out of India's standard financial disclosures the way the
project's existing XBRL collector does for income-statement items.

**Data needed to test in India:** A mapping of listed supplier firms to their major listed
customers. Realistic candidate sources, none of which are a clean bulk feed: (a) sector-specific
public knowledge where relationships are well known and stable (e.g., auto-ancillary firms'
investor presentations routinely name their OEM customers — Bharat Forge, Motherson, etc. list
Maruti/Tata Motors/M&M by name; IT-services firms sometimes disclose top-client revenue
concentration bands but rarely names); (b) company investor-presentation PDFs, which often name
key clients even though the Ind AS 108 note does not; (c) export/import customs shipment data
(each shipment record names both shipper and consignee), usable for export-oriented
manufacturing supply chains specifically. Building this would be a genuine, sector-by-sector
manual/semi-manual curation effort — realistically scoped to a handful of concentrated sectors
(auto ancillaries, possibly select industrials) rather than a market-wide automated collector,
more comparable in effort to the mutual-fund-breadth collector than to a paid-subscription
data-availability question.

**NOT YET TESTED — requires the project's permutation-test gauntlet before any deployment
consideration.**

## Candidate 2: Analyst forecast dispersion (differences-of-opinion) anomaly

**Description:** The cross-sectional standard deviation of sell-side analysts' earnings
forecasts for the same stock, scaled by the absolute mean forecast (or price). Stocks with the
highest dispersion in analyst opinion earn lower subsequent returns than otherwise similar
stocks; low-dispersion minus high-dispersion is a positive-return spread.

**Sources:**
- Diether, K. B., Malloy, C. J., & Scherbina, A. (2002), "Differences of Opinion and the Cross
  Section of Stock Returns," *Journal of Finance*. US evidence: a strategy buying low-dispersion
  and selling high-dispersion stocks produces a statistically significant and economically large
  spread over 1-3 month horizons; the effect is concentrated in small stocks and stocks that
  have performed poorly over the prior year.
- Johnson, T. C. (2004), "Forecast Dispersion and the Cross Section of Expected Returns," *JF*
  — offers a competing risk-based (rather than differences-of-opinion) explanation for the same
  empirical pattern, i.e. the interpretation of *why* dispersion predicts returns is genuinely
  contested in the literature, not settled.
- The DMS dispersion result has been the subject of numerous follow-on and non-US tests since
  2002 (per the broader citing literature), but I did not find a specific, citable India or
  clean broad-emerging-market replication in this search — treat "widely cited/tested" as true
  of the US/developed-market literature, and the India-specific gap as unconfirmed either way,
  not as a negative result.

**Mechanism:** Same differences-of-opinion / short-sale-constraint logic as the mutual-fund
ownership-breadth candidate proposed in the 2026-09-13 run (Chen-Hong-Stein): when investors
disagree about a stock's value and pessimists cannot short it, price reflects the optimists'
view disproportionately, and the stock is more likely to be overpriced. This candidate is a
**different data type** (sell-side analyst forecasts, not fund-holding counts) built on the
**same underlying economic mechanism** as last week's candidate — worth stating explicitly:
this is not a fully independent idea, it is a second empirical proxy for the same theoretical
story, using a different, cheaper-to-obtain data source. If both were ever tested, they should
be expected to be correlated, not treated as two independent confirmations.

**Evidence strength:** Moderate. DMS is a very well-cited, foundational US result, but (a) its
own economic interpretation is directly disputed by a competing published explanation (Johnson
2004's risk-based story), and (b) I could not confirm a specific India or broad-EM academic
replication in this search — this is a genuine "US founding study, contested mechanism, no
confirmed India evidence" case, weaker than Candidate 1's multi-market replication.

**Confound risk (explicit, and unusually direct here):** DMS's own paper states the effect is
"most pronounced in small stocks" — this is close to a textbook restatement of the exact
size/beta confound that killed this project's profitability factor. Any India test of this
candidate should be treated as guilty of the same confound until proven innocent, with the same
explicit size and beta controls used in that postmortem applied from the first test, not as an
afterthought.

**Why it might transfer to India, and why it might not:** India's structural short-sale
constraint is broader than the US's (only ~180-200 F&O-eligible names can be shorted at all,
versus the essentially open US securities-lending market DMS's sample drew on), which — if the
differences-of-opinion mechanism is the true driver rather than Johnson's risk-based
alternative — should make the effect stronger in India, not weaker (the same logic already used
for last week's breadth candidate). Working against transfer: India's sell-side analyst
coverage is thin outside large- and some mid-cap names, so a dispersion measure requiring
multiple independent forecasts per stock is only computable for a meaningfully smaller universe
than the full listed market — likely a comparable-sized universe to the F&O-eligible list,
which would make it hard to cleanly separate "differences of opinion" from "is even covered by
several analysts at all," itself a proxy for size/visibility.

**Data needed to test in India:** Per-stock, per-period consensus estimate detail (individual
analyst estimates or at minimum the cross-sectional standard deviation of estimates, plus
number of contributing analysts, as of each historical date — point-in-time, not
today's snapshot). LSEG/Refinitiv I/B/E/S is the standard institutional source globally but is
a paid, institutional-grade feed with (per available information) historically limited India
coverage before ~2011 — not confirmed as realistically obtainable for this project. A cheaper
domestic alternative exists: Trendlyne's "Consensus Estimates" / Forecaster product aggregates
estimates from 40+ Indian brokerages and shows current analyst counts (e.g. 36 analysts for a
large-cap like Nestlé India) for free, but — consistent with this project's own prior
data-availability check on Trendlyne for the promoter-pledge candidate
(`data-sources/promoter-pledge-data-availability.md`) — historical/point-in-time export of that
data is expected to sit behind the same paid GuruQ tier already flagged as a pending
subscription decision, not a new one. This candidate would likely piggyback on that same
decision rather than requiring an entirely separate data-sourcing effort.

**NOT YET TESTED — requires the project's permutation-test gauntlet before any deployment
consideration.**

## Investigated this run, not proposed

- **Employee stock option (ESOP) grant intensity as a management-alignment signal.** Real US
  literature exists (extensive-ESO-grant IPOs outperform over the following two years; option
  compensation to non-executives correlates with strong prior stock performance), but the
  return-predictability evidence found is concentrated around the IPO event specifically, not a
  general ongoing cross-sectional signal, and no emerging-market or India-specific
  return-predictability study was found — the India-specific material available discusses ESOP
  *prevalence* in the startup/tech ecosystem, not a return effect. Too thin to propose.
- **Employee sentiment from crowdsourced reviews (Glassdoor-style; AmbitionBox is the closest
  India equivalent).** Real, multi-paper US literature exists (employer-rating changes predict
  future returns in several studies), but the direction of the effect is directly disputed in
  the literature itself — at least one 2023 paper finds high employee satisfaction predicts
  *lower*, not higher, future returns, the opposite sign from the earlier "satisfied employees
  = good news" studies. Combined with materially thinner review volume on India-focused
  platforms than Glassdoor has for the US corporate universe (likely usable only for large,
  well-known employers), this doesn't clear the bar this week. Worth revisiting if a future
  scout run finds the sign dispute resolved or an India-specific study.
- **Single-stock options variance risk premium / implied-volatility term structure as a
  cross-sectional equity signal.** India has real academic literature on implied volatility
  dynamics, but it is overwhelmingly index-level (India VIX / Nifty options), not a
  documented cross-sectional single-stock return-predictability study. Combined with the
  already-flagged narrow F&O-eligible universe (~180-200 names) that constrained the
  options-skew candidate proposed in the 2026-08-21 run, this doesn't add a new, evidenced
  angle beyond what's already on the queue from that run.
