## 1. Mandatory CSR-spending shortfall/compliance as a governance signal

**Source(s):**
- Section 135, Companies Act 2013 (India) — the statutory mandate itself: eligible companies
  (net worth ≥ Rs 500 cr, or turnover ≥ Rs 1,000 cr, or net profit ≥ Rs 5 cr in the preceding
  year) must spend at least 2% of average net profit of the preceding three years on approved
  CSR activities, or disclose reasons for shortfall ("comply or explain" until FY2021, now a
  harder mandate with unspent amounts required to be transferred to a specified fund/escrow).
  India was the first country to legislate a mandatory CSR-spending floor.
- Manchiraju, H. & Rajgopal, S., "Does Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) Create
  Shareholder Value? Evidence from the Indian Companies Act 2013," *Journal of Accounting
  Research*, 2017. https://law-journals-books.vlex.com/vid/does-corporate-social-responsibility-855629303
  — finds mandated CSR spending is associated with a *negative* stock-price reaction and
  lower returns, consistent with CSR being a wealth transfer away from shareholders rather
  than value-creating, using firm-level data around the mandate's introduction.
- A ~165-firm BSE-listed study (secondary source, exact citation not independently pinned
  down beyond the search summary) reports an *insignificant* (not negative) relationship
  between mandatory CSR spend and stock returns, but a reduction in systematic risk (beta).
- Dharmapala, D. & Khanna, V., "The Impact of Mandated Corporate Social Responsibility:
  Evidence from India's Companies Act of 2013," CESifo Working Paper, 2016.
  https://www.cesifo.org/en/publications/2016/working-paper/impact-mandated-corporate-social-responsibility-evidence-indias
  — a difference-in-differences design showing the mandate caused a real increase in CSR
  activity (not just relabeling), which is useful background but doesn't itself test returns.
- Data source for testing: the IEPF's public CSR data explorer
  (https://iepf.gov.in/content/csr/global/master/home/ExploreCsrData/company-wise.html) and
  a third-party aggregator, Dataful (https://csr.dataful.in/about-data), both built from
  MCA21's mandatory CSR-2 e-form filings (notified Feb 2022, applicable from FY2020-21
  onward), which contain company-wise *prescribed* 2% CSR obligation vs. *actual* amount
  spent — i.e., a ready-made shortfall/excess ratio, not something that needs to be
  hand-computed from footnotes.

**Claimed effect / markets / periods:** India-only (there is no equivalent mandate anywhere
else to replicate against, which cuts both ways — no cross-market replication is possible in
principle, but also no "US result that might not transfer" risk, since this is the one place
in the world where the signal exists at all). The strongest single study (Manchiraju &
Rajgopal, JAR 2017, data from the mandate's 2014-15 introduction window) finds a negative
return effect; a weaker, less-identified secondary source finds a null effect with a lower-risk
side effect instead. The literature is genuinely split on sign, not just on significance.

**Mechanism:** Two competing, both economically coherent, stories already present in the
literature itself: (a) "CSR-as-tax" — mandated spending is a wealth transfer that reduces
distributable profit without compensating signalling value once it's compulsory rather than
voluntary (voluntary CSR literature generally finds positive signalling effects; forcing it
removes the signal), consistent with Manchiraju & Rajgopal's negative finding; (b) "shortfall
reveals weak governance" — a firm that persistently *under*-spends its mandated 2% (rather
than the absolute spending level) may be revealing cash-flow stress, weak board oversight, or
auditor/regulatory risk-tolerance that a simple compliance-ratio sort could pick up
independent of which of (a) or (b) dominates at the full-compliance end. This second framing —
sorting on *shortfall intensity*, not absolute CSR spend — has not been directly tested by
either study found and is the more promising construction for a return factor, since it
reframes the question from "does CSR spending help or hurt" (where the evidence conflicts) to
"does non-compliance signal something bad" (closer to a governance/red-flag factor, the same
family as the already-queued promoter-pledging and KAM candidates).

**Evidence strength:** Mixed directly on point (one negative, one null/mixed), both from
single-study sources rather than a replicated literature, and neither tests the
shortfall-ratio framing suggested above — they test "CSR spend level" against returns around
the 2014-15 mandate's introduction, which is closer to an event study than a standing
cross-sectional factor backtest. This is explicitly a weaker evidence base than e.g. the
already-queued KAM or capacity-overhang candidates — flagging it clearly rather than
overselling it.

**Why distinct from this project's queue:** Not the same as the already-investigated-and-
rejected BRSR/ESG disclosure-score candidate (2026-09-27, found null) — that was a composite,
largely qualitative disclosure *score*; this is a single quantitative, mandatory, audited
expenditure ratio (prescribed vs. actual CSR spend) with a specific legal compliance
mechanism and consequence (unspent amounts must now be escrowed/transferred, not just
disclosed) behind it. Also distinct from governance-score candidates (IiAS, 2026-09-27,
disclaimed by its own publisher) and from the related-party-transaction/tunneling family
(2026-09-06) — this is a cash expenditure obligation, not a disclosure-quality or
intercompany-transaction measure.

**India transfer and confounds:** N/A for "does it transfer" since it's India-only by
construction. The confound to check first, given this project's profitability-factor
postmortem: larger firms more easily meet the 2% floor (compliance cost is a smaller fraction
of a bigger profit base, and bigger firms have more PR/reputational incentive to comply
visibly), so a naive "shortfall = bad" sort risks just re-discovering a size factor from the
other direction. This needs the same explicit size/beta control that killed profitability,
built in from the start rather than checked after a promising-looking raw result.

**Data needed and availability:** Company-wise prescribed-vs-actual CSR spend is directly
available from MCA21 CSR-2 filings via the IEPF portal and/or the Dataful aggregator, covering
FY2020-21 onward (Dataful's own documentation flags data-quality issues — duplicate records,
inconsistent company names, CIN changes over time — that would need cleaning). This is a
shorter history than the project's existing XBRL income-statement collector (~2018 onward),
so coverage would start from roughly the same window as the already-flagged filing-delay and
KAM candidates. Not yet verified: whether the Dataful aggregator's cleaned CIN-mapped version
is free or paywalled — unlike the Trendlyne pledge-data check done for an earlier candidate,
this has not been checked directly against the live site this run.

**NOT YET TESTED — requires the project's permutation-test gauntlet before any deployment
consideration.**

## 2. Currency/FX-exposure magnitude as a priced risk factor (BRIC evidence incl. India)

**Source(s):**
- Kuchin, I., Elkina, M. & Dranev, Y., "The Impact of Currency Risk on the Value of Firms in
  Emerging Countries," *Journal of Corporate Finance Research*, 13(1), 2019, pp. 7–27 (HSE).
  https://cfjournal.hse.ru/article/view/10071 (could not fetch full text directly this run —
  this project's network tooling could not resolve cfjournal.hse.ru or cyberleninka.ru, so
  the findings below are drawn from search-engine abstract/summary text, not the primary PDF
  — flagged explicitly as unverified against the original the way earlier runs flagged the
  Canada/Malaysia KAM claims and the India climate-risk paper).
- Related/earlier working paper by the same research group: Dranev, Y. & Babushkin, M.,
  "Asymmetric Exchange-Rate Exposure in BRIC Countries," HSE Working Paper WP BRP 27/FE/2014.
  https://wp.hse.ru/data/2014/02/04/1329381211/27FE2014.pdf

**Claimed effect / markets / periods:** Firm-level sample of exchange-listed companies across
Brazil, Russia, India, and China (BRIC), sample period reported as roughly 2003–2013. The
paper models currency risk as a priced factor in an implied cost-of-equity framework (not a
realized-return cross-sectional sort — an important methodological distinction from most of
this project's existing queue). Reported pattern: in Russia, firms with large currency
*losses* carry a *positive* risk premium (standard compensation-for-risk story, concentrated
in exporters). In Brazil, India, and South Africa (the South Africa detail comes from a
companion/related paper in the same search results, not necessarily this exact paper — flagged
as a secondary, less-certain claim), firms with large currency gains *or* losses — i.e., sorted
on exposure magnitude in either direction, not sign — show a *lower* required return than
firms with near-zero exposure. That is a non-monotonic, U-shaped relationship, which is an
unusual shape for a risk-premium story and is exactly the kind of pattern that deserves
skepticism rather than automatic acceptance.

**Mechanism:** The risk-premium framing (channel a: large exposure, in either direction, means
more total risk, which should raise required return, i.e. the opposite sign from what's
reported for India) is the standard story and doesn't fit the reported India sign. The paper's
own likely explanation, going by the "exposure puzzle" literature this sits inside (Jorion 1990
— most firms individually show no significant priced FX exposure in developed markets at all),
is closer to a cash-flow/hedging-quality channel: firms with large, *visible* exposure (import/
export-heavy multinationals, IT-services exporters, commodity-linked firms) are plausibly the
same firms sophisticated enough to hedge well and to be well-covered by analysts, so the
"large exposure" sort may really be picking up a hedging-quality or analyst-coverage proxy
rather than currency risk being priced directly — this is speculative on my part, not stated in
the sources, and should be treated as a hypothesis to test for, not a finding.

**Evidence strength:** Single research group (same HSE authors across the two linked papers),
non-top-tier outlet (a university's own corporate-finance journal, not a general finance
journal), old sample window (2003–2013, missing the entire post-2013 rupee volatility regime
including 2013's own taper-tantrum rupee crisis, 2018-19, and the 2020-22 COVID/war period),
implied cost-of-equity methodology rather than realized trading returns, and an internally
flagged sign inconsistency in the search summary for the South Africa result specifically.
This is weaker-pedigree evidence than most of what's currently on this project's open queue —
flagged plainly rather than oversold, and primary-source verification is still outstanding.

**Why distinct from this project's queue:** Genuinely new data type and new risk-factor
family — not price/technical (closed door), not an accounting ratio, not ownership/flow data,
not a corporate-action announcement. It's the first candidate this scout has proposed built
from a firm's realized/estimated sensitivity to currency movements as a priced characteristic.
Distinct from the already-queued/rejected "FII headroom scarcity" candidate (2026-10-18, a
segmentation/limits-to-arbitrage story about foreign *ownership caps*) and from "single-stock-
futures basis" (2026-08-30, a cost-of-carry signal, not a currency-risk signal) — this is about
a firm's *operating* exposure to INR movements (import/export/commodity intensity), not a
market-structure or derivatives-pricing construct.

**India transfer and confounds:** This is already an India-specific finding (one of the four
BRIC markets directly studied), so the usual "does a US result transfer" question doesn't
apply — the open question is instead whether a decade-old, single-group, non-mainstream-outlet
result with an unusual non-monotonic sign would survive re-estimation on current India data at
all. Rupee volatility and India's trade exposure have both changed materially since 2013
(larger IT-services export base, larger commodity-import bill, a managed-float regime that has
itself evolved), so even a real 2003-2013 effect might not be stable. The obvious confound,
beyond the hedging-quality hypothesis above: currency-exposure magnitude plausibly correlates
with firm size and sector concentration (large-cap IT exporters, large-cap commodity importers)
in ways that would need the same size/beta control that undid the profitability factor.

**Data needed and availability:** Needs (a) a measure of each firm's currency exposure — the
simplest version is a rolling-window regression of stock returns on USD/INR (or a trade-
weighted basket) returns, computable directly from price data the project already has, though
that reintroduces a price-based estimation step into an otherwise "new data type" candidate;
a cleaner fundamentals-based alternative would use segment-wise export/import revenue and
cost breakdowns from annual-report notes (not yet in the XBRL collector's current income-
statement-only scope) to construct a trade-exposure ratio instead of a return-based beta. The
latter is more defensible as a genuinely new data type but is a heavier lift; the former is
feasible today but blurs the line with the closed-door price/technical space and would need
care in how it's framed and tested.

**NOT YET TESTED — requires the project's permutation-test gauntlet before any deployment
consideration.**

## Update to an already-queued candidate (not a new candidate — not counted toward this
## week's total)

**Options-implied skew (queued 2026-08-21):** that entry flagged "strong US evidence... no
known India stock-level replication" as the open gap. A 2026 India-specific study has since
appeared: Shukla, A.C., Khatwani, Mistry, Mitra & Agrawal, "Equity Option Implied Volatility
Skew as a Substitute Credit Risk Signal: Evidence from Negative Rating Announcements in
India," *Investment Management and Financial Innovations*, Issue 5(18), 2026.
https://businessperspectives.org/journals/investment-management-and-financial-innovations/issue-518/equity-option-implied-volatility-skew-as-a-substitute-credit-risk-signal-evidence-from-negative-rating-announcements-in-india-2024-2025
This tests 42 negative credit-rating actions on Nifty 500 F&O-listed stocks, Jan 2024–Nov
2025, and reports that higher pre-event 25-delta put-call skew predicts a deeper post-
announcement price decline (roughly 0.315% more decline per unit of skew). This only partly
closes the gap: it's an event-conditional (credit-downgrade) study, not a general standing
cross-sectional skew factor, so the original candidate's "no India replication" caveat is now
better stated as "no India replication for a general factor; one narrow event-conditional
India confirmation exists." Still sits in the open queue at its original status — this is
informational, not a status change.

## Investigated, not proposed

- **Merger/acquirer long-run post-acquisition underperformance** (the US Loughran-Vijh-style
  anomaly) — real, well-known U.S. and some EM literature, but the India-specific evidence
  found is directly contradictory: some studies report Indian acquirers gain in the long run
  (post-takeover BHAR positive), others (mostly cross-border or IPO-firm-acquirer subsamples)
  find underperformance, and a 2023 India-China cross-border study's specific return findings
  couldn't be confirmed from search snippets alone. Too unsettled in-market to propose.
- **Contingent-liabilities disclosure magnitude as a hidden-risk factor** — found a valuation
  study (IIMB dissertation) showing contingent liabilities are priced negatively in simple
  panel regressions, and one Journal of Behavioral Finance paper treating them as an
  "operational uncertainty" indicator correlated with excess returns, but no actual
  cross-sectional forward-return backtest exists in either source, and neither is India-
  specific. Evidence base too thin and not return-predictability-shaped to propose.
- **Tender-offer buyback announcement returns, specifically post-2023** (distinct from the
  already-rejected open-market-buyback-drift candidate, since SEBI phased out the open-market
  route entirely by April 2025, leaving tender offers as essentially the only buyback
  mechanism in India today) — the only academic event study found is a 2016 paper using a
  2004-2013 sample, predating the entire post-2023 regulatory and tax regime change (buyback
  proceeds became taxable to shareholders from October 2024, which plausibly changes investor
  response to future announcements). No post-2023 study exists yet to say whether the old
  result still holds under the new tax treatment. Flagged as a real gap, not proposed this
  week for lack of current evidence.
- **Corporate bond credit-spread level/change as a leading indicator for the same issuer's
  equity returns** — found only studies of what determines India corporate bond spreads
  (macro variables, liquidity), and a US-focused study on equity-bond anomaly spillover
  (Chordia, Goyal, Nozawa, Subrahmanyam & Tong), but nothing testing this specific
  cross-market linkage on Indian data. No citable India evidence to propose from.
- **Retail-investor sentiment/flow data from discount brokers (Zerodha, Groww, Angel One
  active-client and demat-account counts)** — a genuinely novel India-specific alt-data type
  (broker-level active account and flow data, now with clear secular swings — e.g., reported
  ~40% YoY drop in new demat openings in 2025), but no academic or rigorous quantitative study
  found linking firm-level or even market-level versions of this data to forward returns; only
  news commentary and broker PR. No evidence base to propose a factor from, though flagged as
  a data type worth revisiting if a study appears.
- **Related-party-transaction disclosure and stock-price crash risk** (Selarka & Choudhury,
  NSE sample 2005-2012, later published as a 2023 Springer chapter) — found disclosure lowers
  crash risk, but this is a *downside-risk* claim about disclosure quality, not a *return*
  factor, and sits too close to the already-investigated RPT/tunneling-intensity candidate
  (2026-09-06, found no return-predictability evidence) to count as a sufficiently distinct new
  angle. Not proposed.
