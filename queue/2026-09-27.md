# Literature Scout — 2026-09-27

Two candidates this week. Both are genuinely new data types relative to everything logged in
`INDEX.md`/`REJECTED.md` so far (7 prior runs, spanning options-implied signals, promoter/insider
transactions and pledging, FII/DII and mutual-fund ownership, analyst estimates and dispersion,

## Candidate 1: Political connections (board-level politician ties / corporate political donations)

**Description:** A firm-level characteristic — not a trading signal — capturing whether a company has
structural ties to elected politicians: either a director/board member who is a sitting or former
MP/MLA/state minister, or a documented pattern of corporate political donations (directly, or via
electoral bonds/electoral trusts).

**Sources:**
- Ganguly, Mishra & Platt, "Do Political Connections Pay? Evidence from India," *Emerging Markets
  Finance and Trade* 59(10), 2023. https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/1540496X.2023.2212839
- Awasthi, Yayavaram, George & Sastry, "Political connections and profit persistence in India,"
  *Asia Pacific Journal of Management*, 2025. https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10490-024-09945-5
- "Politically connected firms and access to credit: Evidence from India," *Journal of International
  Financial Markets, Institutions and Money*.
  https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0954349X24000420
- "How do political connections of firms matter during an economic crisis?" and the plain-language
  summary at VoxDev: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304387825000227 ,
  https://voxdev.org/topic/institutions-political-economy/how-political-connections-shield-firms-during-economic-crisis
- Background on the underlying global literature this sits inside (not India-specific, cited for
  mechanism context only): Fisman (2001, Indonesia), Faccio (2006, 47-country cross-section).
- Data-availability context: Association for Democratic Reforms (ADR) compiles Indian corporate
  political donation disclosures, https://adrindia.org/ ; background on the Feb 2024 Supreme Court
  ruling striking down the anonymous electoral-bonds scheme and forcing retroactive donor-party
  disclosure: https://time.com/6696481/india-electoral-bonds-supreme-court/

**Mechanism (source claims):** The claimed mechanism is rent-seeking / preferential-access, not risk
compensation or behavioral bias. Connected firms are argued to get faster/easier licenses, land and
permits (state-level politicians), better access to bank credit (especially via government-owned
banks), and protection during adverse regulatory action or economic crises. The Awasthi et al. paper's
specific angle — that state-level political ties matter *more* than national-level ties because
day-to-day regulatory friction (licenses, land, infrastructure) is a state-government function in
India — is a plausible, India-institution-specific refinement of the generic "political connections"
story, not just an import of the US/Indonesia result.

**My assessment of evidence strength:** This is the best-corroborated candidate surfaced by this
scout so far in terms of within-India replication: at least four independent India-specific empirical
papers (2022-2025), using different designs (director-politician panel matching, election-donation
event studies, crisis difference-in-differences, credit-access regressions), converge on the same
directional finding. That is more independent India confirmation than most prior candidates in this
queue had. However, all of it is single-country (India), and I have not found a paper that runs a
clean India-only cross-sectional *return* spread (long unconnected / short connected or vice versa)
with a Fama-MacBeth-style test — the existing papers mostly test profitability persistence, credit
access, investment behavior and crisis resilience, not a monthly-rebalanced return factor directly.
So "does a connected-minus-unconnected portfolio actually earn a return spread net of costs" is not
yet answered by the literature I found; it would need to be assembled from these accounting/behavioral
results, which is a real gap, not a minor one.

**Confound risk — flagged explicitly, this is the main reason to be cautious:** Politically connected
firms in India skew heavily toward large, established business groups (the kind of firms that can
afford to cultivate political ties in the first place). That is the *opposite*-direction version of
the confound that killed the profitability factor in this project's own testing — there, the "factor"
turned out to be a small-cap/high-beta proxy; here, the risk is that "political connections" is really
a large-cap/business-group-affiliation proxy, which is its own separate literature (this project
already investigated related-party-transaction/tunneling intensity in the 2026-09-06 run and found no
return-predictability evidence — a signal that business-group effects in India don't automatically
translate into a tradeable factor). Any test of this candidate needs an explicit size and
business-group-affiliation control from day one, not as an afterthought.

**Why it's a genuinely new angle vs. prior runs:** The 2026-08-21 and 2026-08-23 candidates
(promoter/insider PIT transactions, promoter share pledging) are about a firm's *own promoter's
trading/financing behavior*. This candidate is a different data type entirely — the firm's
*external political relationships* — with a different mechanism (rent-seeking/access, not
information asymmetry or leverage/crash risk).

**Data needed for an India test:**
- *Politician-director variant:* board-of-director rosters (from annual reports / BSE-NSE corporate
  governance disclosures) cross-matched by name against ECI rolls of MPs/MLAs/state ministers. No
  clean bulk feed exists for this — it is a from-scratch entity-matching effort, comparable in size to
  the customer-supplier-mapping problem flagged in the 2026-09-20 run.
- *Donation variant (cheaper to obtain):* Section 182 Companies Act disclosures of aggregate political
  contributions, as compiled by ADR. This is more bulk-obtainable, but has a known data gap: donations
  routed through electoral bonds (2018 –Feb 2024) had donor-party pairing hidden by design; the
  Feb 2024 Supreme Court ruling forced retroactive disclosure, so a reasonably complete panel is now
  plausible, but pre-2024 electoral-bond-era donations were effectively invisible until that ruling and
  the reconciled dataset's quality/completeness has not been independently verified by me.

NOT YET TESTED — requires the project's permutation-test gauntlet before any deployment consideration.

## Candidate 2: Carbon emissions / climate-transition and physical climate risk as a return factor

**Description:** Two related but distinct constructs from the climate-finance literature: (a) a
carbon-emissions/"transition risk" factor (higher-emitting "brown" firms priced to compensate investors
for future carbon-regulation risk), and (b) a physical climate-risk factor (exposure to heat,
flooding, monsoon disruption). Both are new data types for this project — nothing about emissions or
climate exposure has been tried yet.

**Sources:**
- Bolton & Kacperczyk, "Global Pricing of Carbon-Transition Risk," *Journal of Finance*, 2023 (NBER WP
  28510). https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jofi.13272 ,
  https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28510/w28510.pdf
- Zhang, "Carbon Returns across the Globe," *Journal of Finance*, 2025.
  https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jofi.13402
- India-specific: "Pricing the Heat: A Dynamic Nexus Between Physical Climate Risk and Stock Returns,"
  *Emerging Markets Finance and Trade* 62(2), 2025/2026.
  https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/1540496X.2025.2540874 (full text was blocked by this
  session's network egress proxy — the assessment below relies only on the abstract/summary returned by
  search, not a direct read of the paper; flagging this explicitly rather than passing it off as
  verified)
- "Do stock returns respond to physical and transition climate risks? Evidence from emerging BRICS
  economies." https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0301479724032894
- Critique of the core emissions-premium claim: Aswani, Raghunandan & Rajgopal, "Are Carbon Emissions
  Associated with Stock Returns?" (2023/2024 exchange with Bolton & Kacperczyk) — summarized via
  https://www.ecgi.global/sites/default/files/working_papers/documents/doesthecarbonpremiumreflectriskormispricing.pdf
- Data-availability context: SEBI's BRSR (Business Responsibility and Sustainability Report) mandate
  and reporting-compliance gap — background via
  https://www.ceew.in/gfc/publications/Corporate-Emissions-Disclosures and industry BRSR-compliance
  summaries.

**Mechanism — and why this one is different from a clean "new factor" pitch:** This is not a
settled anomaly with an agreed mechanism; it's an active, unresolved fight in the literature's own
home market (the US). Bolton & Kacperczyk's claim is a forward-looking transition-risk premium.
Aswani, Raghunandan & Rajgopal directly rebut it: the premium disappears when emissions are scaled by
revenue (i.e., using carbon *intensity* instead of raw absolute emissions) or when using
company-disclosed rather than modeled/estimated emissions figures — which is exactly the kind of
"looks like alpha until you control for the obvious thing" pattern that killed the profitability
factor in this project's own testing (there it was size/beta; here critics argue it may just be a
sector/scale proxy, e.g., heavy industry vs. services). Bolton & Kacperczyk have pushed back again, so
this is genuinely contested at JoF level, not a fringe objection.

**My assessment of evidence strength:** Mixed. On the positive side: real multi-market replication
(Zhang 2025 finds the carbon-return relationship holds across many countries and is *larger* in
emerging than developed markets, and there is a BRICS-specific paper plus an India-specific physical
climate-risk paper — so this clears the "more than one country, more than one paper" bar). On the
negative side: the mechanism is disputed even in the founding US evidence, and I could not verify the
India paper's methodology or exact effect size myself (network access to the full text was blocked) —
I'm reporting a search-engine abstract summary, not something I've read and can vouch for in detail.
That's a materially weaker evidence base than Candidate 1's, and I want that asymmetry to be visible
rather than presenting both candidates as equally solid.

**Why it might/might not transfer to India:** Physical climate risk (heat, monsoon, flooding) is
plausibly the more India-relevant half of this literature, since India's economy has real
monsoon-dependent and heat-exposed segments (agriculture-linked consumption, outdoor labor, certain
manufacturing). The transition-risk/emissions half is on weaker footing for India specifically: it
depends on markets pricing in a *future carbon-regulation* cost, and India does not yet have a binding
economy-wide carbon price (a Carbon Credit Trading Scheme was notified in 2023 but is still being
phased in), which weakens the theoretical case for a transition-risk premium existing in India today,
independent of whether the U.S. version of the claim holds up.

**Data needed for an India test:**
- *Emissions/transition variant:* SEBI's BRSR mandates Scope 1 & 2 disclosure for India's top 1,000
  listed companies by market cap, but actual compliance is incomplete — only ~781 companies reported
  Scope 1/2 for FY2023-24 despite the mandate, and the regime has only really existed since roughly
  FY2022-23. That means any point-in-time emissions panel for India is short (2-3 years) and
  survivorship-biased toward larger, more compliant firms — a real constraint given this project's
  usual multi-year permutation-test standard, separate from the mechanism dispute above.
- *Physical-risk variant:* would require mapping company facility locations to a
  heat/precipitation/flood-exposure dataset — a from-scratch geocoding effort with no existing bulk
  feed, comparable in effort to the satellite/GST-type ideas already flagged as build-from-scratch in
  prior runs, rather than something that piggybacks on data already in hand.

NOT YET TESTED — requires the project's permutation-test gauntlet before any deployment consideration.

## Investigated, not proposed this week

- **Satellite/alternative imagery (nightlights, container-port throughput, retail parking-lot car
  counts) as an entry signal.** There is real, well-cited firm-level US precedent (satellite
  parking-lot car counts predicting retailer earnings surprises), and country/macro-level nightlights
  and container-port literature. But I found no India-specific firm-level return study, the
  US precedent applies to a narrow firm type (large retailers with visible parking lots), and the
  closest India-relevant application I found (an ECB paper tracking ~500 cement plants globally via
  satellite) is an industry-activity nowcasting tool, not a tested stock-return factor. Commercial
  satellite-data access is also likely a real cost barrier for a small paper-trading project. Not
  proposed as a candidate — too speculative and too far from "obtainable" this week — but worth
  revisiting if a future run finds an India- or EM-specific firm-level backtest.
- **SEBI enforcement-action reaction as a leading indicator.** Evidence is thin and internally
  inconsistent: one source (India's NIPFP/Leap blog, citing peer-reviewed-adjacent work) reports *no*
  significant price reaction to insider-trading enforcement orders specifically, contradicting the
  standard developed-market (SEC) finding; another source claims large abnormal-return effects for
  fraud/cheating and payment-default charges, but that source is a lower-tier journal (IJCRT) whose
  methodology I have not been able to vet. Given the direct contradiction between sources on the same
  underlying question, this isn't well-diligenced enough to propose as a candidate.
- **UPI/digital-payments transaction data as a revenue-nowcasting alt-data signal for consumer-facing
  listed companies.** Genuinely novel and India-specific (UPI's transaction scale is not replicated
  anywhere else), but I found no academic study linking UPI or NPCI transaction data to equity return
  predictability — the existing literature is about payments-system adoption and financial inclusion,
  not stock selection. NPCI does not publish merchant- or company-level breakdowns. This would be a
  from-scratch data-construction effort with zero existing evidence base, in the same category as the
  GST e-way-bill idea already flagged as investigated-not-proposed in the 2026-08-30 run.
