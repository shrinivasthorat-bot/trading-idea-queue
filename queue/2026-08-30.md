## Candidate 1: Media coverage / investor-attention ("neglected firm") effect

**What it is:** Rank stocks by the volume of media/news coverage they receive (count of news
articles or press mentions, independent of the tone/sentiment of that coverage), and hypothesize
that low-coverage ("neglected") stocks earn higher subsequent returns than high-coverage stocks.
This is a distinct construct from both (a) the technical/price indicators already closed by this
project, and (b) the "aggregate news sentiment" work already looked at for India (which measures
tone, not attention/volume, and found only short-lived index-level effects — see 2026-08-23 run's
implicit coverage of sentiment work).

**Source(s):**
- Fang, L. & Peress, J., "Media Coverage and the Cross-Section of Stock Returns," *The Journal of
  Finance*, 2009. https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2009.01493.x /
  https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=971202
- Chinese-market replication: "Media Coverage and the Cross-Section of Stock Returns: The Chinese
  Evidence," https://www.researchgate.net/publication/324578280 , and a related "Media sentiment
  and cross-sectional stock returns in the Chinese stock market,"
  https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0275531921002117
- Wikipedia summary of the broader "neglected firm effect" literature (Arbel & Strebel and
  follow-ons): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neglected_firm_effect
- GDELT Project (candidate free/open data source for constructing a media-attention variable
  globally, including India): https://blog.gdeltproject.org/ ; academic use of GDELT for
  market-sentiment construction, including an emerging-markets-inclusive 35-country study (2006–
  2020) finding media-sentiment effects "robust in both developed and emerging market subsamples"
  (source-reported, not verified by us).

**Claimed effect (source's claims, not verified by us):** Fang & Peress report that stocks with no
media coverage earn higher returns than stocks with high media coverage even after controlling for
standard risk factors (size, book-to-market, momentum, liquidity), with the effect "more pronounced
among small stocks and stocks with high individual ownership, low analyst following, and high
idiosyncratic volatility." Search summaries describing the paper's own robustness section state the
effect "remains robust even after controlling for firm size and book-to-market," i.e. the authors
claim it is not simply a repackaged size premium — but we have not read the regression tables
ourselves, only a secondary description of them, so this claim should be verified against the
primary paper (or its published replication packages) before being taken as settled. The effect has
been replicated directionally in China, a genuinely different (non-US, emerging/still-developing)
market, which is a meaningfully stronger evidentiary base than a single-country result.

**Mechanism:** Media coverage is a channel of information dissemination; when a stock is not
covered, information about it reaches fewer investors, takes longer to be reflected in price, and
the stock is more exposed to a limited-attention/limited-market-participation risk premium
(investors need to know a stock exists before they can buy it — a Merton (1987) style incomplete-
information mechanism). This is economically distinct from the momentum/technical patterns this
project has already closed, and distinct from the sentiment-tone work already looked at — it is
about *how many people are paying attention*, not what the news says or what the price has done.

**Our assessment of evidence strength:** Moderate. The original result is from a top-tier journal,
economically motivated (not a pattern search), and has at least one credible replication in a
different market (China) using different data. That is a real evidentiary base, not a single
fragile study. However: (1) we found **no India-specific study** of this effect — we are relying
on the assumption that a US/China result transfers, which is exactly the kind of unverified
transfer-assumption this scout is supposed to flag rather than pass through; (2) the effect is
explicitly reported as "more pronounced among small stocks... and low analyst following," which is
uncomfortably close to the small-cap/high-beta signature that turned out to be the entire story
behind this project's profitability-factor rejection (REJECTED.md item 2) — the authors' claim that
it survives a size/BM control is not the same as this project's own more thorough size-and-beta
confound test, and until someone runs that test on India data, this should be treated as
"plausible, not yet ruled out as a size proxy," not as "confirmed distinct from size."

**Why it might or might not transfer to India:** India has a large population of thinly-covered
small/mid-cap listed names (much of the NSE-listed universe outside the Nifty 500 gets little to no
sell-side or press coverage at all), which is the raw material this effect needs to exist. But
India's retail investor base, media landscape (regional-language press, financial Twitter/YouTube
as major distribution channels not captured by English-language news counts), and market
microstructure differ enough from both the US and China that the size of any effect is unknown, not
just untested. This is a market where the confound risk (this being yet another size/liquidity
proxy) needs to be checked first, not last.

**What data would be needed:** A time series of media-mention counts per NSE-listed company. This
is realistically obtainable without a new expensive vendor relationship: GDELT (the Global Database
of Events, Language, and Tone) is free, covers Indian English-language and some vernacular outlets,
is queryable via Google BigQuery, and has already been used in published academic work to construct
market-level and firm-level attention variables. A simpler, cruder proxy — sell-side analyst count
per stock per quarter — is also usable and may already be available from the same aggregators used
for the promoter-pledge candidate (Trendlyne, screener.in-style sources). Either way this is a new
data pipeline relative to the XBRL fundamentals collector, but a low-cost one (GDELT is free; the
analyst-count proxy needs no new subscription).

**NOT YET TESTED — requires the project's permutation-test gauntlet before any deployment
consideration.**

## Candidate 2: IPO lock-up expiration as a short-window selling-pressure signal

**What it is:** Around the date a post-IPO share lock-up expires (the date on which promoters,
anchor investors, or other pre-IPO shareholders first become legally free to sell), track the stock
for a mechanical, temporary negative-return / liquidity-shock window driven by the release of a
large supply overhang, independent of any new information about the company.

**Source(s):**
- Field, L.C. & Hanka, G., "The Expiration of IPO Share Lockups," *The Journal of Finance*, 2001,
  56(2), 471–500. https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/0022-1082.00334 /
  https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=205011
- China replication (liquidity/microstructure channel): "The impact of initial public offering
  lockup expirations on liquidity: Evidence from Chinese stock market," *Journal of Shanghai
  Jiaotong University (Science)*, https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s12204-016-1702-7.pdf
- Malaysia replication (abnormal volume/spread, 379 IPOs, 2001–2011, per search summary) — cited
  secondhand via search results; original paper not independently located/verified this run.
- India context (market commentary only, not an academic study): reporting on the ~$4bn of IPO
  lock-ups that expired in India in early 2025, e.g.
  https://www.business-standard.com/markets/news/stock-market-braces-for-over-3-5-billion-of-expiring-ipo-lockups-125021000243_1.html
  — useful for confirming the phenomenon is large and tracked in India, not as evidence of a
  return effect.

**Claimed effect (source's claims, not verified by us):** Field & Hanka, using 1,948 lock-up
agreements, report a three-day abnormal return of roughly −1.5% (a related version of the result is
cited elsewhere as a −1.8% cumulative abnormal return over the expiry week) around the expiration
date, together with a **permanent** ~40% increase in average trading volume from that point on. The
effect is reported as materially larger when the firm was venture-capital-backed, attributed to VCs
selling more aggressively than executives or other insiders. The China and Malaysia studies located
report a consistent direction (increased abnormal volume/spread, and in the Chinese case a
liquidity deterioration tied to disclosure quality) rather than an identical effect size — treat
these as directional replications, not exact-magnitude confirmations.

**Mechanism:** This is a mechanical supply-shock story, not a behavioral-bias or private-information
story: a large block of shares that was legally locked becomes sellable on a known, pre-announced
date, and if the newly-freed holders (especially financially motivated pre-IPO investors, PE/VC
funds, or anchor investors under SEBI's 30/90-day rules) sell into the market, price falls to clear
the temporary excess supply before recovering (or not) once the overhang is absorbed. This is a
different mechanism from every other candidate this project has evaluated: it requires no
information advantage and no behavioral mispricing, just a scheduled, foreseeable liquidity event —
which also means the *dates* themselves are public and known well in advance, unlike most of the
signals proposed so far.

**Our assessment of evidence strength:** Moderate-to-strong on mechanism and multi-market
replication (US original + directionally consistent findings in China and Malaysia, two different
emerging-market microstructure environments), but **no India-specific academic study was located**
— only financial-press commentary confirming the phenomenon exists and is economically large in
India, not confirming a tradable return pattern. Unlike Candidate 1, this mechanism does not depend
on investor psychology or information frictions that might plausibly differ across markets — a
supply/demand shock from forced or motivated selling should transfer relatively mechanically to any
market with lock-up rules and available insiders willing to sell, which India clearly has (SEBI
mandates lock-ups similar in spirit to the US/China regimes: 18 months for the mandatory minimum
promoter contribution, 6 months+ for other pre-IPO shareholders, 30/90 days for anchor investors).
That said, "the mechanism should transfer" is still our own assessment, not a verified India
result — flagging that distinction explicitly, per this scout's brief.

**A framing issue specific to this project (read before testing):** This project is long-only. A
negative abnormal return around a known future date is not directly a long-entry signal — it is
either (a) a reason to reduce or exit an existing long position ahead of a known lock-up date (an
avoid/de-risk overlay, structurally similar in role to the promoter-pledge candidate from
2026-08-23, though the underlying mechanism is completely different), or (b) speculative
justification for a long entry shortly *after* the expiry-date drop, betting on mean-reversion once
the overhang clears — but that second use is not what Field & Hanka document; they document the
drop and the permanent volume increase, not a subsequent reversal. Testing this properly means
deciding up front which of these two very different trades is being evaluated, rather than treating
"lock-up expiry" as a single undifferentiated signal.

**What data would be needed:** For each India IPO: the listing date, the lock-up terms and
expiration date(s) for each shareholder class (available from the IPO prospectus / RHP, which is a
public SEBI filing), and daily price/volume data around each date. IPO lock-up schedules for Indian
listings are already tracked by financial media and by data providers who publish "upcoming lock-up
expiries" calendars (as seen in the market-commentary sources above), so the event-date data itself
looks readily obtainable; the harder part is systematically extracting lock-up terms per shareholder
class from each prospectus at scale, which is a new, prospectus-parsing pipeline distinct from the
XBRL fundamentals collector.

**NOT YET TESTED — requires the project's permutation-test gauntlet before any deployment
consideration.**

## Investigated but not proposed this week

**Firm-level institutional ownership change (as distinct from aggregate FII/DII flow).** Prior runs
(2026-08-21, 2026-08-23) looked for and failed to find a citable India stock-level study of FII/DII
*flow* predicting returns; this run specifically looked for a different, more granular construct —
firm-level *changes in institutional ownership* using quarterly shareholding-pattern disclosures
(Regulation 31), the same underlying filing type already used for the open promoter-pledge
candidate. We found three distinct, apparently inconsistent pieces of evidence and are recording
the inconsistency rather than resolving it: (1) Arora (2016, *Global Business Review* 17(3),
654–664) is widely summarized as finding "DII investment has a significant positive relation with
future stock returns" — but based on how it is described in every secondary source we could find,
this appears to use aggregate national FII/DII net-investment flow data (the same kind of data
already investigated and rejected in prior runs), not firm-level holdings, so it may not be a new
result at all; we could not confirm this either way because journals.sagepub.com was blocked to
direct fetch. (2) A genuinely firm-level India panel study exists — quarterly institutional holding
data for 1,591 firms, 2001–2015, ~95,460 firm-quarters (ScienceDirect, 2017) — but as best we can
tell from search summaries, it studies what firm characteristics institutions prefer (a determinants
study), not whether ownership changes forecast subsequent returns, so it doesn't directly support or
refute this candidate either. (3) The most rigorous cross-country evidence we found — Dyakov &
Wipplinger, "Institutional Ownership and Future Stock Returns: An International Perspective,"
*International Review of Finance*, 2020 (13,807 funds, 16 countries, 2001–2014) — reports weak
evidence at best, and by some specifications a null or negative relationship, between changes in
institutional ownership and subsequent stock returns internationally. Given that the single most
rigorous, broadest-sample source we found points toward "this effect is weak or doesn't exist"
rather than confirming it, and we could not verify a clean India-specific firm-level result either
way, we are not proposing this as a candidate. Recorded here so a future run does not re-spend time
on it without either (a) confirming Arora's actual data granularity, or (b) finding an India-specific
study that directly tests ownership-change-predicts-returns at the firm level.

**GST e-way bill volume as macro/sector alternative data.** On the "untried data types" list in
spirit (genuinely new, India-specific administrative data). E-way bill volume is well-established
in policy/economics literature as a macro nowcasting indicator for GDP and GST collections, but we
found no academic or practitioner study connecting firm- or sector-level e-way bill activity to
equity return predictability. Absent any evidence that this translates into a stock-selection edge
(as opposed to a macro/economic-activity indicator), we are not proposing it; flagging it here as an
idea that would need a foundational empirical case built essentially from scratch before it clears
this scout's bar of "a documented external finding to evaluate," which is a materially higher lift
than the other candidates on this queue.

**Single-stock-futures basis / cost-of-carry mispricing.** Investigated as a genuinely India-
specific, F&O-data-driven candidate distinct from the already-open options-skew signal. The one
directly relevant study found — Pathak, Verousis & Chauhan (2017), *Journal of Emerging Market
Finance* (per SAGE listing) — examines the pricing error between a single-stock future's implied
spot price and the actual spot price on NSE, and is described (in the secondary source we could
access) as finding that this pricing error "contains incremental information about stock returns...
of day t, and not beyond." That is a same-day, contemporaneous information-content result, not a
forward-looking predictive signal a systematic strategy could act on — the finding as reported gives
no basis for a next-day-or-later entry signal. Not proposing; recorded so a future run doesn't
re-open this without a source that documents actual forward predictability rather than
same-day information content.
