## Candidate 1: Abnormal reporting timeliness / filing-delay anomaly

**Description:** A firm's earnings/annual-report filing date, measured not in absolute days but
as a *surprise* relative to the firm's own historical filing pattern (or relative to the
regulatory deadline). Unexpectedly late filers underperform; the effect is asymmetric — firms
sitting on bad news delay disclosure more than firms with good news.

**Sources:**
- Bagnoli, M., Kross, W., & Watts, S. G. (2002), "The Information in Management's Expected Report
  Date: A Day Late, A Penny Short," *Contemporary Accounting Research*. Finds unexpectedly late
  (and unexpectedly early) earnings announcements produce a negative market reaction even after
  controlling for the earnings surprise itself, and that bad-news firms report later than
  good-news firms.
- "Abnormally long audit report lags and future stock price crash risk: Evidence from China"
  (ResearchGate; China A-share evidence) — abnormal audit-report lag predicts elevated future
  crash risk, consistent with the same bad-news-hoarding mechanism outside the US.
- General cross-country "determinants of reporting lag" literature (e.g. French evidence,
  Aubert et al.) documents that reporting lag correlates with firm complexity, risk, and weak
  profitability across multiple markets, though most of that strand studies the *determinants*
  of lag rather than return predictability per se.
- "Are Lengthy Audit Report Lags a Warning Signal?" (Audit Analytics / *Current Issues in
  Auditing*, AAA) and a separate study linking abnormal audit lag to a higher probability of
  future restatement — both US evidence, both consistent with "long unexplained delay = bad
  news is coming."

**Mechanism:** Straightforward information-economics story, not a data-mined pattern: managers
control the timing of mandatory disclosure within a regulatory window, and have an incentive
(career concerns, avoiding an immediate price hit, buying time to manage the message) to delay
release of bad news while releasing good news promptly. The market, in turn, should partially
price in "no news yet" as itself informative once a filing is later than the firm's own typical
cadence or the statutory deadline. This is the same logic as post-earnings-announcement drift's
underlying "information not yet fully impounded" story, but keyed off *disclosure timing* rather
than the earnings number itself — a different, non-price data axis.

**Evidence strength:** Moderate-to-good. The core finding (Bagnoli-Kross-Watts) is a well-cited,
multi-decade-old US result, and the mechanism (bad news delayed) has been independently
corroborated in China via the crash-risk channel — genuine multi-market replication, which is
more than several candidates in this queue's own prior entries could claim. It is not, however,
as heavily replicated as momentum or value; I found no meta-analysis or large out-of-sample
replication and no India-specific academic study testing return predictability from filing
timeliness (only the general "determinants of lag" literature, which doesn't test returns).
Treat "no India study found" as absence of evidence, not evidence of absence — it may simply be
an under-mined data type in Indian academic finance, not one that was tried and failed.

**Confound risk (explicit, given this project's own history):** The"determinants of lag"
literature also finds reporting lag is correlated with firm size, complexity, and profitability —
i.e., naively using *absolute* filing lag as a signal risks reconstructing exactly the
small-cap/high-beta proxy that killed the profitability factor in this project's own testing.
The defensible version of this signal is a *within-firm surprise* measure — this quarter's lag
vs. this firm's own trailing filing-lag history (or lag vs. the firm's own sector/deadline
norm) — which nets out a large part of the cross-sectional size/complexity level effect by
construction. Any test of this candidate should be run both on the naive absolute-lag version
and the within-firm-surprise version, and should include the same size/beta controls that
exposed the profitability-factor confound, precisely because the failure mode looks so similar.

**Why it might transfer to India:** SEBI's LODR Regulation 33 gives every listed company a hard,
public, and *uniform* deadline (45 days for quarterly results, 60 days for annual) and — since a
2023 SEBI circular — requires companies to disclose the specific reason for any delay to the
exchanges. That regulatory structure is arguably cleaner than the US disclosure regime for this
purpose: the "expected" filing date is not something that has to be estimated from analyst
consensus (as in the original US studies) but is a known, common, exchange-mandated deadline,
making "how many days early/late relative to the deadline, and relative to this firm's own past
behavior" a well-defined, easily computed variable.

**Why it might not:** India's discretionary "delay window" is narrower than in the US (60 days
max vs. the US's effectively unbounded-but-penalized late-10-K regime), so there may simply be
less cross-sectional variance in filing lag to exploit outside of the minority of firms who file
right at or past the deadline. It's also possible enforcement/exchange-technology mechanics (e.g.
board-meeting intimation timing) dominate the true information content in ways the US studies
don't have to model.

**Data needed to test in India:** Exact filing date of each quarterly/annual result per company,
per period, matched to (a) the statutory deadline for that period and (b) that firm's own
historical average lag. NSE and BSE both publish free, unauthenticated corporate-announcement
feeds (NSE's "Corporate Filings – Financial Results" page; BSE announcements) with per-filing
timestamps, updated intraday, and this project's own XBRL point-in-time collector already
ingests these income-statement filings from ~2018 onward for its primary purpose — meaning the
filing *date* needed for this candidate is very plausibly already sitting in existing collector
metadata (or capturable with a small addition to it) rather than requiring new infrastructure.
This is the cheapest data-acquisition case in this queue to date, if the collector is confirmed
to retain submission timestamps.

**NOT YET TESTED — requires the project's permutation-test gauntlet before any deployment
consideration.**

## Candidate 2: Mutual-fund ownership breadth (differences-of-opinion / short-sale-constraint proxy)

**Description:** The number (or fraction) of distinct mutual fund schemes holding a stock, and
especially the *change* in that count quarter-over-quarter — as opposed to the aggregate rupee
value of fund ownership. Chen, Hong & Stein's original construct: falling breadth signals that
opinion is becoming more divided and that short-sale constraints are increasingly binding,
predicting a subsequent overpricing correction (i.e., low future returns), while breadth
increases carry no equivalent one-sided signal.

**Sources:**
- Chen, J., Hong, H., & Stein, J. C. (2002), "Breadth of Ownership and Stock Returns," *Journal
  of Financial Economics* (NBER WP 8151). Using US mutual-fund holdings 1979-1998, stocks in the
  lowest decile of breadth *change* underperform the highest decile by 6.38%/yr gross,
  4.95%/yr after controlling for size, book-to-market and momentum.
- Follow-on literature (e.g. a later *Review of Finance* piece, "What Does Stock Ownership
  Breadth Measure?") has since debated whether breadth is really capturing short-sale
  constraints/differences-of-opinion specifically, or is partly just an investor-recognition
  (visibility) effect — i.e., the mechanism is contested even in the US, not settled.

**Mechanism:** In a market with short-sale constraints, pessimists who can't act on their view
are simply priced out of the marginal trade; only optimists' valuations get reflected in price.
When the pool of *willing holders* (breadth) shrinks, that is itself informative that the
marginal holder is becoming more optimistic relative to the average investor, i.e. the stock is
more likely overpriced relative to consensus fundamental views. This is a genuinely different
economic story from a pure ownership-level or flow-pressure effect (the FII/DII flow-induced-
pressure candidates already investigated and not proposed in the 2026-08-21 run) — it is about
the *number of distinct opinions represented in ownership*, not the *magnitude* of ownership or
of a flow shock.

**Evidence strength:** Single well-known study as the founding result, with a real, unresolved
methodological debate afterward about whether breadth is really measuring the short-sale/
differences-of-opinion story or something closer to investor recognition. I did not find a
clean India-specific or broader emerging-market replication using granular fund-level breadth
(as distinct from the aggregate FII/DII/institutional-ownership work already investigated in
this queue's prior runs, which use ownership *level* or *flow*, not breadth/count-of-holders).
This is meaningfully weaker evidence than Candidate 1 — one primary study plus an open academic
argument about interpretation, not a replicated-across-markets finding.

**Why it might transfer to India, and why it's a distinct angle from prior queue entries:**
Cash-market short selling is effectively unavailable to Indian retail investors, and stock-level
derivatives (hence any real ability to express a short view) exist for only ~180-200 F&O-eligible
names out of 1,500+ listed companies. That means short-sale constraints structurally bind far
more broadly across the Indian listed universe than in the US, where the CHS sample period
already had reasonably liquid options/short markets for large names. If the CHS mechanism is
real, India's non-F&O universe is arguably close to an ideal setting for it — a much larger set
of stocks where pessimists genuinely cannot express their view except by not holding.

**Why it might not (confound risk, stated explicitly):** This is close to the same trap as the
profitability factor. The set of stocks with structurally low or falling breadth is very likely
to overlap heavily with the small-cap, low-liquidity, low-institutional-attention segment — the
same segment whose "profitability premium" collapsed into a size/beta proxy once controlled for.
A breadth-of-ownership signal that isn't tested with explicit size and liquidity controls from
day one should be assumed guilty of the same confound until proven otherwise.

**Data needed to test in India:** Every mutual fund scheme's month-end portfolio holdings, per
SEBI's mandatory monthly portfolio-disclosure requirement (all ~40+ AMCs are required to publish
full scheme holdings monthly, and AMFI aggregates links to these disclosures at
amfiindia.com under "Other Data → Scheme-wise disclosure of investments"). This is a new,
previously-untried data type for this project — free in principle, but each AMC publishes its
own PDF/Excel file separately, so building a point-in-time panel of "number of distinct schemes
holding stock X as of month-end T" is a real scraping/normalization effort, comparable in scope
to what the XBRL collector already required for financials (i.e., realistically obtainable, but
not a quick add — budget for a dedicated collector, not a side-query).

**NOT YET TESTED — requires the project's permutation-test gauntlet before any deployment
consideration.**

## Investigated this run, not proposed

- **Annual report readability / Fog index.** Real, multi-paper literature exists, including
  India-specific work ("Annual report readability and stock return synchronicity: Evidence from
  India," *Cogent Economics & Finance* 2023) and a US mechanism paper (Lawrence 2013, *JAE*)
  tying poor readability to retail-investor mispricing (~91bps per std. dev. of readability in
  US data). Not proposed because the India study found so far tests *synchronicity*, not forward
  return predictability, so there is no India evidence yet that this converts into a tradeable
  return spread rather than just an information-environment descriptive fact. Text-complexity
  scoring of Indian annual reports (Fog index requires parsing MD&A text out of PDFs across
  years) is also a non-trivial data-engineering lift. Worth revisiting if a future scout run
  turns up an India study that directly tests forward returns rather than synchronicity.
- **FII/DII net positioning in index futures and options (participant-wise OI data).** NSE
  publishes free daily participant-wise open interest splits (FII/DII/Pro/Client) in index
  futures and options, and retail commentary treats FII long-short ratios as a market-timing
  signal. Not proposed: this is fundamentally an index-level, market-timing dataset (net
  positioning in *Nifty* futures/options), not a stock-level cross-sectional signal, and this
  project's mandate is long-only stock selection, not index timing. It's also not clear any of
  the retail-commentary "signal" claims for this data have been through any rigorous academic
  test at all — what I found was practitioner narrative, not peer-reviewed evidence.
