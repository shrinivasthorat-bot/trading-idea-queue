## Candidate 1: Options-implied volatility skew ("smirk") as a cross-sectional entry signal

**What it is:** For each stock with listed options, compute the "smirk" — the spread between
implied volatility (IV) of out-of-the-money (OTM) puts and at-the-money (ATM) calls/puts on that
stock. Stocks with the steepest smirks (relatively expensive downside protection) are hypothesized
to underperform stocks with flat/shallow smirks going forward.

**Source(s):**
- Xing, Zhang & Zhao, "What Does Individual Option Volatility Smirk Tell Us About Future Equity
  Returns?", *Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis*, 2010.
  https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1107464 /
  https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-financial-and-quantitative-analysis/article/abs/what-does-the-individual-option-volatility-smirk-tell-us-about-future-equity-returns/ECFD16BA9ACBDC8D577D1BD866FBEA72
- Surrounding literature corroborating an options→equity informed-trading channel: Cremers &
  Weinbaum (volatility spread), and a 2025 explanatory piece, "Why does options market information
  predict stock returns?", ScienceDirect: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304405X25001618
- India-specific: a Nifty-index-level study, "Study of Implied Volatility Skew In Indian Stock
  Market" (ResearchGate, 2006–2015 sample, t-tests): https://www.researchgate.net/publication/362302776_Study_of_Implied_Volatility_Skew_In_Indian_Stock_Market

**Claimed effect (source's claim, not verified by us):** Xing/Zhang/Zhao report the steepest-smirk
decile underperforms the flattest-smirk decile by roughly 10.9%/yr on a risk-adjusted basis in the
US, with the spread persisting for at least six months, and steep-smirk firms subsequently
experiencing the worst earnings shocks. This is a well-cited, frequently-replicated result within
the US options literature (multiple follow-on papers using variants of the volatility-spread
construction reach directionally similar conclusions).

**Mechanism:** Informed traders with negative private information prefer to express that view via
OTM puts rather than shorting the underlying (embedded leverage, avoids short-sale constraints and
uptick-type frictions, bounded loss). That informed put buying pushes OTM put IV up relative to
calls/ATM before the equity market has fully priced the news in, so a steep smirk today functions
as a leading indicator of bad news arriving in the underlying stock. This is a genuinely different
mechanism from anything in the project's closed technical-indicator set — it's about where informed
money chooses to trade, not a price pattern.

**Our assessment of evidence strength:** Moderate-to-strong for the US large-cap options market —
this is not a single fragile study, it spawned a durable sub-literature and the mechanism
(informed traders prefer options under short-sale frictions) is economically coherent and
consistent with other informed-trading findings elsewhere in finance. However, we found no
credible India-specific *cross-sectional, stock-level* replication. The only India evidence located
studies Nifty *index*-level skew predicting *index* returns over 2006–2015 using simple t-tests —
that is a different question (market-timing signal, not stock selection) and a much weaker
methodology (no cross-sectional sorts, no controls, no out-of-sample split reported). So: the
mechanism and the developed-market evidence are solid; whether it holds cross-sectionally on
India-listed single names is genuinely untested, not just under-published.

**Why it might not transfer to India:** NSE lists options on roughly 180+ F&O-eligible stocks
(large/mid-caps only, by SEBI eligibility criteria), and single-stock option chains are
meaningfully thinner than index options — OTM strike liquidity dries up faster, especially near
expiry, and contracts are event-driven around results/sector news rather than continuously quoted.
That means (a) the tradable universe for this signal would be capped at the F&O list, a small
fraction of the NSE-listed universe and likely overlapping heavily with the large-cap names where
the profitability-factor confound (size/beta) already burned this project once, and (b) thin OTM
liquidity could make the IV skew noisy or manipulable at the smaller end of that list, unlike the
deep, continuously-quoted US single-stock options market Xing/Zhang/Zhao used.

**What data would be needed:** Historical daily options chains (strike, expiry, IV or the
raw price to back out IV, open interest) per NSE F&O stock, going back several years. NSE
publishes historical F&O bhavcopy data; IV would need to be reconstructed (Black-Scholes inversion)
or sourced from a vendor. This is a new data pipeline, not an extension of the existing XBRL
fundamentals collector — realistically obtainable but non-trivial engineering effort, and probably
only worth building if the F&O-eligible universe overlap with the project's tradable universe is
large enough to matter.

**NOT YET TESTED — requires the project's permutation-test gauntlet before any deployment
consideration.**

## Candidate 2: Promoter/insider trading, tested strictly on post-disclosure returns

**What it is:** Use SEBI-mandated insider trading disclosures (Prohibition of Insider Trading
Regulations, PIT 2015) — specifically promoter and promoter-group transactions — as a signal:
buy (or overweight) names following promoter net-buying disclosures.

**Source(s):**
- "Do Insider Trades Predict Future Stock Returns? Evidence From Indian Stock Market", *IJLLR*
  (event study, NSE/BSE, 2015–2024 sample, NIFTY 50 benchmark):
  https://www.ijllr.com/post/do-insider-trades-predict-future-stock-returns-evidence-from-indian-stock-market
- "Market Reaction to Insider Trading: Evidence from Regulatory Orders in India", The Leap Blog,
  May 2026: https://blog.theleapjournal.org/2026/05/market-reaction-to-insider-trading.html
- "Regulatory reforms, share buyback and institutional monitoring of insider trading: Evidence
  from India", ScienceDirect (650 dividend-initiation events, 2002–2024):
  https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1544612325018483

**Claimed effect (source's claim, not verified by us):** The IJLLR study reports insider
purchases followed by statistically significant positive abnormal returns, with the effect
"stronger for promoter trades and small-cap firms" — consistent with concentrated ownership and
higher information asymmetry in those segments. It also reports **pre-announcement** abnormal
returns averaging ~6.55% over the (-5,-1) trading-day window and ~6.6–6.7% over (-20,-1),
essentially unchanged full-sample vs. post-PITR-2015 (~5.9%), which the authors interpret as
information leakage ahead of public disclosure.

**Mechanism:** Promoters and other insiders in India typically hold large, concentrated stakes
(India has among the highest promoter-ownership concentration of major markets) and have access to
material non-public information; their trades, once disclosed, are a plausible signal of that
information reaching the market. This is directly a "new data type" the project hasn't tried and
has a textbook-plausible mechanism (information asymmetry + concentrated insider ownership),
not a pattern with no rationale.

**Our assessment of evidence strength — and the critical caveat:** The mechanism is plausible and
there is direct India evidence (not just a US result assumed to transfer), which is better founded
than most candidates we could propose. But the effect sizes reported by the sources we found are
concentrated in the *pre-disclosure* window — the abnormal return has already largely happened by
the time the trade is publicly disclosed, and this doesn't change materially after the 2015
regulatory tightening that was meant to reduce leakage. None of the sources we could access
isolate a clean, standalone post-disclosure-date drift number, which is the only thing a systematic
strategy — which by construction can only observe the disclosure, not the actual trade date — could
capture. This is structurally the same failure mode that killed the profitability factor in this
project: a documented effect that looks like alpha in an event study but may not survive being
reframed as "what can I actually trade on, using only information available at signal time." We are
flagging this explicitly rather than passing the headline number through uncritically. If this is
tested, it must be tested using only forward returns from the disclosure date (not the transaction
date), and the human running the gauntlet should treat this pre/post distinction as the central
question, not a footnote.

**Why it might not transfer / other risks to check:** (a) as above, most of the documented alpha
may sit in a window that isn't tradable off public data; (b) small-cap concentration raises the
same size/liquidity confound the profitability factor had — promoter-trade effects being "stronger
in small caps" should be re-tested controlling for size and beta before being trusted, exactly as
was done for profitability; (c) promoters may trade for many idiosyncratic reasons (pledging,
personal liquidity, succession, tax) unrelated to firm-specific information, which could dilute the
average signal — the sources note insider *sales* in particular show weaker, less consistent
predictive power than purchases, so any strategy should probably be asymmetric (act on buys, ignore
or treat differently sells).

**What data would be needed:** SEBI PIT disclosure filings — promoter/promoter-group identity,
transaction date, disclosure date, transaction type (buy/sell) and value — available via NSE/BSE
corporate announcement feeds or third-party aggregators (e.g. insiderscreener.com surfaces this).
This is a separate data source from the project's XBRL fundamentals collector and would need its
own ingestion pipeline, but the raw disclosures are public and the format is standardized under
PIT 2015, so this looks realistically obtainable back to at least 2015.

**NOT YET TESTED — requires the project's permutation-test gauntlet before any deployment
consideration.**

## Investigated but not proposed this week

**FII/DII (and other institutional ownership) flow data.** This is on the project's list of
untried data types, so we looked specifically for a rigorous, stock-level, out-of-sample study of
FII/DII flows predicting *individual stock* returns in India (as opposed to market-level
correlation with volatility). We did not find one that met a reasonable bar — the available
material is mostly practitioner/blog-level commentary on FII/DII flows moving the *aggregate*
index, or academic pieces on the dynamic *interaction* between FII and mutual-fund flows rather
than return prediction. One search summary explicitly characterized FII flows as "strong
short-term indicators of sentiment but not reliable long-term predictors" — that is a source's
casual claim, not a citation we can point to with a specific effect size, and we don't want to pass
it off as evidence. Worth revisiting in a future run with more targeted search once we know what
academic search terms/venues actually cover India stock-level flow data, but not proposing it now.

**Mutual-fund flow-induced trading pressure (demand-shock) signals.** This is the more rigorous
cousin of raw FII/DII flow — using disclosed fund holdings plus fund-level flows to build a
stock-level measure of non-fundamental buying/selling pressure (Coval-Stafford / Dong Lou-style
"flow-based return predictability", originally US). We flag this mainly because of what we found
*against* it: Wardlaw (2020, *Journal of Finance*), "Measuring Mutual Fund Flow Pressure as Shock
to Stock Returns," shows the standard construction of this measure is mechanically contaminated —
it is inadvertently a direct function of the stock's own realized return — and that once that
artifact is removed, "many established results in this literature no longer hold." That is
structurally the same lesson as this project's own profitability-factor postmortem (an effect that
turned out to be a proxy for something else once the confound was controlled for), coming from
within the literature's own top journal. Given that the flagship result underlying this idea has
already been substantially walked back by a rigorous follow-up, and we could not find India-market
holdings/flow data availability confirmed, we are not proposing it this week. Recorded here so a
future run does not re-propose it without addressing the Wardlaw critique head-on.

**Analyst estimate revisions.** Also on the untried-data-types list. We found general
developed-market evidence of an analyst-forecast-revision drift ("PFRD") and one mention that
price impact of revisions differs between developed and emerging markets for *positive* revisions
specifically (no difference detected for negative revisions), but no India-specific study with a
concrete effect size we could cite. Sell-side analyst coverage of India small/mid-caps is also
likely thin, which would limit the useful universe even if a signal existed. Not proposing this
week for lack of a citable India-relevant result; worth a more targeted search next time (IBES/
Refinitiv India coverage, or India-specific brokerage research aggregators).
